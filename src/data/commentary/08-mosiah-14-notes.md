Chapter context: Mosiah 14 is Abinadi's unbroken quotation of Isaiah 53,
delivered mid-trial before King Noah's court. No other chapter in the
Book of Mormon is expounded as immediately or as closely by the chapter
that follows it.

## WORLD BEHIND THE TEXT

1. **A quotation inside a trial.** Abinadi stands bound before Noah's court
   (Mosiah 12–13). He has already indicted the priests as teachers who do not
   practice what they profess and has recited the commandments to them; this
   chapter is the scriptural centerpiece of his defense, delivered without
   interruption.
2. **The proof-text duel.** The priests opened the interrogation with Isaiah
   52:7–10 — 14:1 w3–6 "doth not Isaiah say" answers their "what meaneth the
   words" challenge by continuing the very scroll they quoted. Isaiah 53
   follows directly on Isaiah 52 in sequence; Abinadi's rebuttal is not a
   change of subject but an extension of their own citation to its
   conclusion.
3. **Brass-plates Isaiah.** The chapter tracks the King James text of Isaiah
   53 nearly word for word, with a small set of real variants (see PHRASE
   LEVEL). Whether those variants reflect the brass-plates text or the
   translation's register cannot be settled from the text alone; the notes
   below flag both readings where it matters.
4. **A sacrificial audience.** Noah's priests profess the law of Moses
   (Mosiah 12:28). Isaiah's 14:10 w19–24 "his soul an offering for sin" lands on
   hearers whose religion is built around offerings — the poem forces the
   question of what their altars point to.
5. **Court prophets and courts.** The scene of a lone prophet answering a
   hostile royal court over a temple-adjacent message has a close biblical
   shape in Jeremiah's trial (Jer. 26), where priests likewise demand death
   and the defense is a recitation of the Lord's words.

## DEPTH: WORD LEVEL

### "comeliness" (v. 2)
Attractiveness of form or bearing. The servant's introduction strips him of
every visual credential a court like Noah's would honor — no royal beauty,
nothing that would make an audience 14:2 w39–40 "desire him". In a throne room
built on display (Mosiah 11:8–9), the word choice is itself an indictment.

### "esteemed / esteem" (vv. 3–4)
To appraise, to set a value on. The pair of uses runs the poem's central
error in miniature: 14:3 w30–33 "we esteemed him not" — a failed appraisal — and
then 14:4 w12–19 "we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God" — a confident,
wrong one. Human judgment does not merely miss the servant; it actively
mis-prices him, reading his suffering as divine rejection when it is divine
assignment.

### "dumb" (v. 7)
Silent, not mute by defect. The sheep 14:7 w27–31 "before her shearers is dumb"
by disposition; the servant's silence is chosen restraint under legal
provocation — the one behavior a trial cannot process. Abinadi, by contrast,
will not stop talking; the contrast between the text he reads and his own
conduct is deliberate: his commission is to speak, the servant's was to
submit.

### "travail" (v. 11)
Labor in its hardest sense, including birth-labor. 14:11 w4–8 "the travail of his soul"
plants a birth image inside an execution text: the suffering is generative,
and its offspring is named in the previous verse — 14:10 w25–29 "he shall see his seed".
The exposition in the next chapter builds its whole doctrine of spiritual
descent on that pairing.

### "intercession" (v. 12)
Advocacy on behalf of another, a standing-between. The poem's last word-idea:
the executed man ends as legal counsel 14:12 w46–48 "for the transgressors" who
killed him. This is the term Abinadi's own exposition reaches for first —
Mosiah 15:8 gives the Son "power to make intercession" — making it the load-
bearing word of the whole sermon.

## DEPTH: PHRASE LEVEL

### "Yea, even doth not Isaiah say:" (v. 1)
Abinadi's splice — the only non-Isaiah words in the chapter. Framing the
quotation as a question turns scripture into cross-examination: the priests
asked what Isaiah 52 *meaneth*; Abinadi answers by asking whether they have
read the next column. The formula also names the source outright, which is
rare in Book of Mormon quotation practice and marks this as citation, not
allusion.

### Modernized verb forms: "has borne... opened... bore" (vv. 4, 7, 12)
14:4 w1–6 "Surely he has borne our griefs" (KJV "hath"),
14:7 w32–37 "so he opened not his mouth" at the verse's second instance (KJV
"openeth"), and 14:12 w37–40 "he bore the sins" (KJV "bare") — three register
updates toward modern forms. Yet the same chapter retains "hath" three
times (14:2 w20–25 "he hath no form nor comeliness", v. 6, v. 10). The
inconsistency is the observation: the text is not systematically modernized,
which fits occasional translation smoothing better than a distinct source
text.

### "with his stripes we are healed" (v. 5)
The line later scripture cannot leave alone: Peter quotes it of Christ's
body on the tree, and Alma's Zarahemla sermon language of being "healed"
belongs to its family. Within this chapter it completes the exchange
structure of verse 5 — wounded / our transgressions, bruised / our
iniquities, chastisement / our peace, stripes / our healing — four clauses,
each trading his injury for our benefit.

### The pluralizing pattern: "iniquities... transgressions... sins" (vv. 6, 8, 12)
Three places where the King James singular becomes plural:
14:6 w24–28 "the iniquities of us all" (KJV "iniquity"),
14:8 w28–32 "the transgressions of my people" (KJV "transgression"), and
14:12 w39–42 "the sins of many" (KJV "sin"). The consistent direction of the
change matters more than any single instance: guilt is counted, not
abstract — a ledger of acts laid on the servant rather than a general
condition. The exposition's insistence that he bears *their* iniquities
individually (15:9, 12) reads like a sermon on the plurals.

### "he had done no evil" (v. 9)
The chapter's one substantive lexical variant from the King James text,
which reads "he had done no violence." "Evil" widens the claim from a class
of acts to moral character in total — the burial-with-the-rich is explained
not by the absence of a crime but by the absence of any fault. Whether the
variant is textual (brass plates) or translational cannot be decided from
this verse alone; what can be said is that the wider claim is the one
Abinadi's argument needs, since his exposition rests on a wholly sinless
substitute.

### "he shall see the travail of his soul" (v. 11)
Where the King James text reads "he shall see *of* the travail," this text
drops the "of." The smallest variant in the chapter carries a real shift:
with the "of," the servant tastes a portion of what his suffering
produced; without it, he sees the whole of it. Satisfaction is total, not representative.

## DEPTH: VERSE LEVEL

**(v. 1)** The frame. Abinadi's question-formula introduces the block, and
the poem's own opening is also a question — 14:1 w7–11 "Who hath believed our report" —
so the chapter begins with two interrogations stacked: the prophet
questioning the court, the text questioning its readers. "Report" is the
prophets' collective testimony; the priests, professional custodians of
that report, are its unbelievers.

**(vv. 2–3)** The unrecognized servant. Botanical smallness
(14:2 w8–11 "as a tender plant", 14:2 w14–19 "a root out of dry ground") and social
rejection (14:3 w3–7 "despised and rejected of men") — the description is
built entirely from negations of what courts value. Verse 3's close,
14:3 w30–33 "we esteemed him not", indicts the speakers, and Abinadi reads it
aloud to men in the act of not esteeming.

**(vv. 4–6)** The substitution core. Six exchanges in three verses: griefs
and sorrows carried (v. 4), wounds and bruises for transgressions and
iniquities (v. 5), and the summary act — 14:6 w18–28 "the Lord hath laid on him the iniquities of us all".
Verse 4 adds the tragic misreading: 14:4 w12–16 "we did esteem him stricken" —
the bearers of the benefit interpret it as the servant's punishment. Verse 6
supplies the poem's only image of the beneficiaries: 14:6 w1–7 "All we, like sheep, have gone astray",
scattered not by rebellion's drama but by each turning
14:6 w13–16 "to his own way".

**(vv. 7–9)** Silent trial, wrong grave. The legal movement: oppression and
affliction met with silence (v. 7, twice — 14:7 w9–13 "he opened not his mouth"
and 14:7 w32–37 "so he opened not his mouth"), removal
14:8 w4–8 "from prison and from judgment", execution
(14:8 w18–26 "cut off out of the land of the living"), and burial among
14:9 w7–12 "the wicked, and with the rich". Verse 8's unanswerable question —
14:8 w10–14 "who shall declare his generation" — a man executed without
descendants — is the hinge the next chapter turns on. Verse 9's grounds for
the strange burial: 14:9 w17–21 "he had done no evil", the variant reading
treated above.

**(vv. 10–12)** The Lord's pleasure and the servant's portion. The poem
pivots on its hardest line — 14:10 w2–8 "it pleased the Lord to bruise him" —
and immediately converts the bruising into offering-language:
14:10 w15–24 "when thou shalt make his soul an offering for sin". What follows
answers verse 8: the childless man 14:10 w26–29 "shall see his seed", the dead
man 14:10 w31–34 "shall prolong his days". Verse 11 grounds the many's
acquittal in 14:11 w14–15 "his knowledge", and verse 12 ends the poem in
paradoxical triumph: a portion 14:12 w8–10 "with the great" won by being
14:12 w32–35 "numbered with the transgressors", closing on the advocate's act —
14:12 w44–48 "made intercession for the transgressors".

## DEPTH: CHAPTER LEVEL

1. **A preserved quotation.** The record keeps the block intact rather than
   summarizing it — twelve verses of source text inside a trial transcript.
   The modern chapter division isolates the poem as its own unit, which
   matches how the narrative treats it: nothing interrupts, and the next
   chapter opens with Abinadi resuming his own voice to expound it.
2. **Rebuttal by continuation.** The priests' chosen text celebrated the
   beautiful feet of the peace-publisher (12:21); Abinadi answers with the
   un-beautiful publisher of peace — no form, no comeliness, no beauty —
   whose 14:5 w15–18 "chastisement of our peace" redefines the word the priests
   quoted. Isaiah 53 continues directly from the passage they chose; the
   whole trial turns on who has read the scroll further.
3. **Structure:** the poem's pronoun architecture does the theological work —
   a "we" that misjudges (vv. 2–6), a "he" who absorbs (vv. 4–9), and an
   "I"/"my" divine voice that owns the plan (vv. 8, 10–12,
   14:11 w17–19 "my righteous servant"). Abinadi exploits the built-in courtroom:
   report, witnesses, verdict are already in the text he reads.
4. **The question the chapter plants.** 14:8 w10–14 "who shall declare his generation"
   is left hanging here on purpose; its answer is the next chapter's seed
   doctrine. Reading Mosiah 14 alone reproduces the experience of Isaiah's
   first audience — the poem without its exposition.

## DEPTH: BOOK LEVEL

1. **Hinge of the Abinadi unit (Mosiah 11–17).** The unit runs accusation
   (11–12), law (12–13), text (14), exposition (15–16), verdict (17). This
   chapter is the pivot from confrontation to doctrine: everything after it
   is commentary on it, and the only chapter of the unit with no narrative
   motion at all.
2. **The chapter with the most immediate commentary.** Mosiah 15 re-asks this
   chapter's question — "who shall declare his generation?" becomes
   Mosiah 15:10 w39–43 "who shall be his seed" — and answers it with
   the prophets and believers as the servant's posterity — an exposition
   that tracks this chapter's own wording phrase by phrase.
3. **Witness through-line.** Abinadi will enact the text he reads: put to
   death without cause, having Mosiah 17:20 w32–40 "sealed the truth of his words by his death".
   The first convert of that death, Alma, becomes the living answer to the
   generation question — seed produced by the witness's offering.
4. **Benjamin's anticipation.** The suffering Messiah's physical anguish in this
   poem was already preached in Zarahemla — blood from every pore, anguish
   for the wickedness of his people (Mosiah 3:7) — so within the book of
   Mosiah the Isaiah text lands as confirmation of an angel's words the
   exile colony never heard.
5. **Law-as-type through-line.** Abinadi has just told the priests that the
   law's ordinances were types of things to come (13:31); this chapter
   supplies the thing to come. The offering-for-sin line converts every
   altar in Noah's city into a pointer at the servant.

## WORLD IN FRONT OF THE TEXT

### Applications

**Mis-esteeming (vv. 2–4).** The poem's speakers are not villains; they are
appraisers using the ordinary criteria — appearance, success, the assumption
that suffering signals God's disfavor. The application is an audit of my
criteria: where I have concluded that someone's affliction is their verdict,
verses 4–5 say the appraisal may be exactly backwards.

**Carried, not counted against (vv. 4–6, 11–12).** The poem's grammar keeps
insisting the burden moved: borne, carried, laid on him, bear their
iniquities, bore the sins. Living inside that grammar means treating my own
forgiven past as genuinely transferred — and extending the same accounting
to people whose wrongs against me I am still carrying as theirs.

### Self-reflection questions

- (v. 3) Whom have I esteemed not — and what credential were they missing
  that the servant also lacked?
- (vv. 4–5) Which of my actual griefs do I believe he has *already* borne,
  and which am I still treating as solely mine?
- (v. 7) When accusation comes, what would it take for me to trust my case
  enough to answer with less?
- (v. 12) Who has transgressed against me for whom I am willing to make
  intercession?
