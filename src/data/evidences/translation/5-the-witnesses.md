# The Witnesses: Formal Testimony and the Test of Time
* image: the-witnesses.webp

## Two Classes of Formal Witness

The Book of Mormon is unique among scriptural texts in carrying, bound into every copy since 1830, the sworn statements of eleven named men attesting to its physical source. The Book of Mormon itself anticipated the arrangement: "in the mouth of three witnesses shall these things be established" (Ether 5:4; 2 Nephi 27:12–13). Scholarship distinguishes the two groups by the nature of their experience: the **Three Witnesses** (Oliver Cowdery, David Whitmer, Martin Harris) testified of a *visionary and auditory* experience — an angel exhibiting the plates and the voice of God declaring the translation correct — while the **Eight Witnesses** (four Whitmers, three Smiths, and Hiram Page) testified of a plainly *empirical* one: Joseph showed them the uncovered plates, which they handled and hefted, examining the engravings, "and this we bear record with words of soberness."

The events are datable to late June 1829: the Three Witnesses' experience in the woods near the Whitmer farm in Fayette (Harris, having withdrawn, received the same manifestation separately with Joseph shortly after), and the Eight Witnesses' examination near the Smith home in Manchester days later.

## The Enduring Strength of the Witnesses' Testimony

The foundational modern study is Richard Lloyd Anderson's *Investigating the Book of Mormon Witnesses* (1981), which subjected each man's life to the historian's standard tests: character, consistency, capacity, and opportunity for exposure. On every test the testimony holds — and it is precisely this durability that any skeptical explanation of the Book of Mormon must find a way around.

**All three of the Three Witnesses left the Church.** In the crisis year of 1838, Cowdery was excommunicated, Whitmer withdrew, and Harris was estranged; each had personal grievances against Joseph Smith and, for years or decades, every social and financial incentive to expose a fraud if fraud there was. **None ever denied the testimony. All reaffirmed it to death.**

**Oliver Cowdery** practiced law in Ohio and Wisconsin for a decade outside the Church, his legal career itself a testament to the credibility he maintained among non-Mormons. Returning to the Saints at Kanesville, Iowa, in October 1848, he declared publicly:

<details>
<summary>"I wrote, with my own pen, the entire Book of Mormon (save a few pages), as it fell from the lips of the Prophet Joseph Smith." — Oliver Cowdery, 1848 <em>(expand for full statement)</em></summary>

> "I wrote, with my own pen, the entire Book of Mormon (save a few pages), as it fell from the lips of the Prophet Joseph Smith, as he translated it by the gift and power of God, by the means of the Urim and Thummim, or, as it is called by that book, 'holy interpreters.' I beheld with my eyes and handled with my hands the gold plates from which it was translated. I also saw with my eyes and handled with my hands the 'holy interpreters.' That book is true... It contains the everlasting gospel, and came forth to the children of men in fulfillment of the revelations of John, where he says he saw an angel come with the everlasting gospel to preach to every nation, kindred, tongue and people. It contains principles of salvation; and if you, my hearers, will walk by its light and obey its precepts, you will be saved with an everlasting salvation." (Reuben Miller journal, October 21, 1848)

</details>

He died in David Whitmer's home in 1850; Whitmer recorded his final words: "Brother David, be true to your testimony to the Book of Mormon."

**Martin Harris**, who had mortgaged and lost his farm to print the book — $3,000, a fortune for a Palmyra farmer — remained decades in Kirtland outside the main body before coming to Utah and rebaptism in 1870. When young William Harrison Homer, visiting him at the Kirtland Temple in December 1869, asked whether he still believed the Book of Mormon was true, Harris answered:

<details>
<summary>"Do I believe it! Do you see the sun shining! Just as surely... do I know that the Book of Mormon was divinely translated." — Martin Harris, 1869 <em>(expand for full testimony and deathbed statement)</em></summary>

> "Do I believe it! Do you see the sun shining! Just as surely as the sun is shining on us and gives us light, and the moon and stars give us light by night, just as surely as the breath of life sustains us, so surely do I know that Joseph Smith was a true prophet of God, chosen of God to open the last dispensation of the fulness of times; so surely do I know that the Book of Mormon was divinely translated. I saw the plates; I saw the Angel; I heard the voice of God. And I do know that Joseph Smith was a Prophet of God, holding the keys of the Holy Priesthood."

Homer was present again at Harris's deathbed in Clarkston, Utah, in July 1875, and recorded his final words:

> "Yes, I did see the plates on which the Book of Mormon was written; I did see the angel; I did hear the voice of God; and I do know that Joseph Smith is a Prophet of God, holding the keys of the Holy Priesthood." (William Harrison Homer, "The Passing of Martin Harris," *Improvement Era* 29, March 1926)

</details>

**David Whitmer** never returned to the Church, which makes him the strongest test case, and he was interviewed more than seventy times, often by hostile or skeptical questioners, over fifty years in Richmond, Missouri — where the local (non-Mormon) press and civic leaders vouched for his integrity in a signed statement. Stung by encyclopedia claims that the witnesses had recanted, Whitmer published a formal proclamation in 1881 and repeated it in his 1887 pamphlet *An Address to All Believers in Christ* — a document written to *criticize* the Latter-day Saint church, yet anchored on the witness's own certainty:

<details>
<summary>"I have never at any time denied that testimony or any part thereof." — David Whitmer, 1887 <em>(expand for full statement)</em></summary>

> "It is recorded in the American Cyclopaedia and the Encyclopaedia Britannica, that I, David Whitmer, have denied my testimony as one of the three witnesses to the divinity of the Book of Mormon; and that the other two witnesses, Oliver Cowdery and Martin Harris, denied their testimony to that Book. I will say once more to all mankind, that I have never at any time denied that testimony or any part thereof. I also testify to the world, that neither Oliver Cowdery or Martin Harris ever at any time denied their testimony. They both died reaffirming the truth of the divine authenticity of the Book of Mormon. I was present at the death bed of Oliver Cowdery, and his last words were, 'Brother David, be true to your testimony to the Book of Mormon.'" (*An Address to All Believers in Christ*, 1887)

</details>

When interviewers suggested he might have been deceived or seen only with "the eye of faith," he was emphatic. In a July 1884 interview at Richmond with Joseph Smith III and others, a Colonel Giles suggested Whitmer might have been moved upon by "some mental disturbance, or hallucination." A witness to the exchange recorded that Whitmer rose to his full height and answered: "No, sir! I was not under any hallucination, nor was I deceived! I saw with these eyes and I heard with these ears! I know whereof I speak!" He died in 1888, reaffirming the testimony days before death before assembled family and his physician.

The Eight Witnesses show the same pattern: those who left the Church (the surviving Whitmers, Hiram Page) continued to affirm they had lifted and examined the plates. John Whitmer, excommunicated in 1838, told Theodore Turley bluntly that year that he had "handled those plates; there were fine engravings on both sides."

## The Informal Witnesses

Beyond the formal eleven stands a second circle of witnesses — men and women who never signed a printed testimony but who observed the translation at close range, handled the covered plates, or, in one remarkable case, were shown them outright. Because they were never organized as witnesses, their independence gives their testimony a distinct evidentiary character: no one selected them, coordinated them, or published them together. Larry E. Morris's *A Documentary History of the Book of Mormon* (Oxford, 2019) and the documents section of Welch's *Opening the Heavens* collect their statements; Scripture Central's evidence summary counts at least a dozen such observers, and notably, **none of them — believer, unbeliever, or apostate — ever claimed to have caught Joseph with a manuscript, a book, or a collaborator.**

**Emma Hale Smith** belongs first to this circle as a scribe and daily observer, but she was also a *tactile* witness to the plates. In her 1879 testimony she recalled:

<details>
<summary>"I once felt of the plates, as they thus lay on the table, tracing their outline and shape." — Emma Smith, 1879 <em>(expand for full statement)</em></summary>

> "The plates often lay on the table without any attempt at concealment, wrapped in a small linen table cloth, which I had given him to fold them in. I once felt of the plates, as they thus lay on the table, tracing their outline and shape. They seemed to be pliable like thick paper, and would rustle with a metallic sound when the edges were moved by the thumb, as one does sometimes thumb the edges of a book... I moved them from place to place on the table, as it was necessary in doing my work." ("Last Testimony of Sister Emma," *Saints' Herald*, October 1, 1879)

</details>

An intimacy of contact no fraud dependent on a hidden or imaginary object could easily survive for months in a small home.

**Mary Musselman Whitmer**, mother of five of the eleven formal witnesses, is sometimes called the "twelfth witness" — and she is the only person known to have been shown the plates by a heavenly messenger *without* being named in the formal testimonies. In June 1829 her household absorbed Joseph, Emma, and Oliver as boarders, and the added labor fell on her. Her grandson John C. Whitmer recorded her oft-repeated account:

<details>
<summary>"She was shown the plates of the Book of Mormon by a holy angel, whom she always called Brother Nephi." — John C. Whitmer, of his grandmother Mary Whitmer <em>(expand for full account)</em></summary>

> "I have heard my grandmother say on several occasions that she was shown the plates of the Book of Mormon by a holy angel, whom she always called Brother Nephi... One evening, when she went to the barn to milk the cows, she met a stranger carrying something on his back that looked like a knapsack. At first she was a little afraid of him, but when he spoke to her in a kind, friendly tone and began to explain to her the nature of the work which was going on in her house, she was filled with inexpressible joy and satisfaction. He then untied his knapsack and showed her a bundle of plates, which in size and appearance corresponded with the description subsequently given by the witnesses to the Book of Mormon. This strange person turned the leaves of the book of plates over, leaf after leaf, and also showed her the engravings upon them; after which he told her to be patient and faithful in bearing her burden a little longer, promising that if she would do so, she should be blessed; and her reward would be sure, if she proved faithful to the end." (John C. Whitmer, recorded 1878)

</details>

David Whitmer independently confirmed his mother's experience in interviews. The account's function is notable: the manifestation came not to authenticate the book publicly but to sustain an overworked woman privately — and she remained a believer in the plates until her death in 1856.

**Elizabeth Ann Whitmer Cowdery**, Mary's daughter, who in 1832 married Oliver Cowdery, watched the translation in her family home as a teenager. In an 1870 signed statement she certified: "I often sat by and saw and heard them translate and write for hours together. Joseph never had a curtain drawn between him and his scribe while he was translating" (statement, February 15, 1870) — a detail significant against later theories of concealment.

**Michael Morse**, Emma's brother-in-law and never a Latter-day Saint, described to RLDS apostle W. W. Blair in 1879 what he had seen in Harmony:

<details>
<summary>"Placing the Seer Stone in the crown of a hat, then putting his face into the hat... dictating word after word." — Michael Morse, 1879 <em>(expand for full description)</em></summary>

> "The mode of procedure consisted in Joseph's placing the Seer Stone in the crown of a hat, then putting his face into the hat, so as to entirely cover his face, resting his elbows upon his knees, and then dictating word after word, while the scribes — Emma, John Whitmer, O. Cowdery, or some other, wrote it down." (W. W. Blair letter, *Saints' Herald* 26, 1879)

</details>

A disinterested party's confirmation, given fifty years later with nothing to gain, of exactly the method the believing witnesses described.

**Joseph Knight Sr.**, the Colesville farmer who repeatedly provisioned the translators with food and paper at critical moments, left the same description in his own untutored hand:

<details>
<summary>"He would take a sentance and it would apper in Brite Roman Letters." — Joseph Knight Sr. <em>(expand for full account, original spelling)</em></summary>

> "Now the way he translated was he put the urim and thummim into his hat and Darkned his Eyes then he would take a sentance and it would apper in Brite Roman Letters then he would tell the writer and he would write it. Then that would go away the next sentance would Come and so on. But if it was not Spelt rite it would not go away till it was rite, so we see it was marvelous. Thus was the hol translated." (Joseph Knight Sr., Reminiscence, ca. 1835–47, original spelling retained)

</details>

**Sarah Heller Conrad**, a young woman working in the Whitmer home, observed the work daily. **Lucy Mack Smith** and **William Smith** handled the plates and instruments through cloth and left detailed descriptions of weight, dimensions, and the breastplate. Even **Isaac Hale**, Emma's hostile father, corroborated the physical method — stone, hat, face covered — in the very affidavit he gave to discredit the work in 1834 (quoted in the Instruments article).

Taken together, the informal witnesses close the evidentiary gaps the formal testimonies leave open. The Three and Eight attest to the plates' reality; the informal witnesses attest to the *process* — its openness, its lack of props, its day-after-day consistency before family, boarders, skeptics, and in-laws. A fraud must deceive not only its audience but its household. This one, on the surviving record, deceived no one close enough to check.

## Assessment

Anderson's conclusion, broadly sustained in subsequent scholarship (Morris 2019; Dan Vogel's skeptical counter-readings notwithstanding), is that the witnesses fail every profile of collusive fraud: they were interviewed separately across six decades, in changing circumstances of belief and estrangement, before friendly and hostile audiences, and their accounts remained materially consistent. Naturalistic explanations must therefore posit hallucination shared in detail among eleven men on multiple occasions — including the flatly tactile experience of the Eight, Emma's thumbed pages, and Mary Whitmer's turned leaves — sustained without defection through poverty, persecution, apostasy, and death. As the concluding line of the Three Witnesses' testimony frames their own understanding of the stakes: "the voice of the Lord commanded us that we should bear record of it; wherefore, to be obedient unto the commandments of God, we bear testimony of these things." The historian can verify what the witnesses did with that charge: every one of them kept it to the grave.
