# Time: The Chronology and Pace of the Translation
* image: time.jpg

## Establishing the Timeline

The chronology of the Book of Mormon translation can be reconstructed with unusual precision because it is anchored by dated documents: revelations (with their dates in the Doctrine and Covenants), a copyright filing, priesthood restoration dates, and multiple journals and reminiscences. The standard scholarly reconstruction is John W. Welch's "The Miraculous Timing of the Translation of the Book of Mormon," in *Opening the Heavens: Accounts of Divine Manifestations, 1820–1844* (2nd ed., 2017), which assembles over two hundred documents bearing on the question, together with a day-by-day chronology of the 1829 work.

The sequence: Joseph obtained the plates **September 22, 1827**, and moved to Harmony, Pennsylvania, that December. Between **April and June 14, 1828**, Martin Harris served as scribe for the translation of the book of Lehi — the **116 manuscript pages** lost through Harris that summer, an episode that halted the work, occasioned the earliest revelations now in the Doctrine and Covenants (D&C 3; 10), and cost Joseph the interpreters for a season. Translation resumed only fitfully; Emma and possibly her brother Reuben Hale scribed small amounts through the winter.

The decisive phase began when **Oliver Cowdery arrived in Harmony on April 5, 1829**, and began writing on **April 7**. From that date the record shows nearly continuous work: the Aaronic Priesthood restoration of **May 15, 1829** (Joseph Smith—History 1:68–72) occurred because the translators reached the subject of baptism in 3 Nephi; the move to the Whitmer farm in Fayette, New York, came about **June 1**; the title page — translated from "the very last leaf" of the plates, as Joseph noted — was deposited with federal clerk R. R. Lansing to secure copyright on **June 11, 1829**; the experiences of the Three and Eight Witnesses followed in late June; and the translation was complete by approximately **the end of June 1829**.

## Sixty-Five Working Days

Welch's calculation: from April 7 to the end of June is about eighty-five calendar days. Subtracting documented interruptions — travel to Colesville and Fayette, securing the copyright, priesthood restorations, preaching and baptisms, business regarding food and paper, Sabbaths — leaves **approximately sixty-five working days**, and plausibly fewer, in which essentially the entire English Book of Mormon as we now have it was dictated.

The arithmetic is stark. The 1830 edition runs about **269,000 words** (roughly 590 printed pages; 531 pages in the current edition). Sixty-five working days yields a sustained average exceeding **3,500–4,000 words — seven to eight current pages — per day**, dictated in final form, without notes or revision. Oliver Cowdery's contemporaneous summary:

The manuscript evidence (see the Manuscript article) confirms the pace: forward-moving dictation with no compositional revision.

## Comparative Context

Comparisons, while inexact, calibrate expectations. The King James Version — a *translation* by roughly four dozen of England's finest linguists working in organized companies from existing texts and prior English versions — required about **seven years** (1604–1611). Professional novelists working with full literacy, outlines, and revision cycles rarely sustain 3,500 finished words daily for three months; those who write quickly revise heavily. The Book of Mormon's pace was sustained without either advantage — no source text before the translator's eyes, and no revision behind him.

## Dictated Out of Order: The Beginning Written Last

One structural fact of the timeline deserves its own treatment, because it converts the chronology from a curiosity into an argument: **the beginning of the Book of Mormon was dictated after the middle and the end.**

The reason lies in the aftermath of the lost 116 pages. In the revelation now standing as D&C 10, Joseph was commanded *not* to retranslate the stolen book of Lehi — enemies had altered the manuscript to spring a trap (D&C 10:10–19) — but instead to translate the small "plates of Nephi," which covered the same period, "down even till you come to the reign of king Benjamin" (D&C 10:41). The scholarly consensus that has emerged from the manuscript and documentary evidence — argued by Royal Skousen from scribal hands on the original manuscript, and adopted by Welch, MacKay and Dirkmaat, and the Joseph Smith Papers editors — is the **"Mosiah-first" sequence**: when Oliver Cowdery began writing on April 7, 1829, Joseph resumed dictating where the lost pages had left off, near the beginning of our present book of Mosiah. Translator and scribe then proceeded straight through Alma, Helaman, 3 and 4 Nephi, Mormon, Ether, and Moroni. Only afterward — largely at Fayette in June, where the hands of additional Whitmer-household scribes appear on the surviving leaves of the small-plates portion — did Joseph dictate 1 Nephi through Words of Mormon, with Words of Mormon serving as the editorial bridge stitching the replacement material to the already-completed abridgment.

The implications are considerable. First, every internal cross-reference between the book's beginning and its later portions ran **backward against the order of composition**. When Alma 3 notes the fulfillment of the "cursing" pronounced upon the Lamanites in the days of Nephi, the prophecy it cites (2 Nephi 5:21–25) had not yet been dictated; it would be spoken into the record weeks later — and would need to match. The same holds for Nephi's and Jacob's programmatic prophecies of Christ's ministry, the scattering and gathering of Israel, and the destiny of Lehi's seed, all of which the already-dictated middle and end of the book presuppose, quote, and declare fulfilled. Second, the already-written portion was **fixed**: it sat inscribed in ink, in scribes' hands, with no practice of revision (see the Manuscript article). Whoever dictated 1 Nephi in June 1829 had to plant, in exact detail and without contradiction, the prophetic foundations for hundreds of pages that could no longer be changed — orally, at speed, without notes. Third, the sequence explains otherwise puzzling features naturally: why the Aaronic Priesthood restoration (prompted by 3 Nephi's baptismal material) precedes the dictation of 1 Nephi; why the title page, taken from "the last leaf" of the plates, was available for the June 11 copyright deposit before the front of the book existed on paper; and why Words of Mormon reads as a self-aware editorial seam. For the historian, the Mosiah-first sequence is a reconstruction; for the question of origins, it is a constraint that any theory of composition must satisfy — and one that ordinary serial authorship satisfies poorly.
