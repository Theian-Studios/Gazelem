Chapter context: Jacob 5 is the longest chapter in the small plates — Zenos's
allegory of the olive trees, quoted whole from the brass plates as Jacob's
answer to the question he posed in the previous chapter. Verse notes cluster
by the allegory's visits to the vineyard, which is how the chapter itself
keeps time.

## WORLD BEHIND THE TEXT

1. **A brass-plates prophet.** Zenos survives only in Book of Mormon
   citation — quoted by Nephi, Jacob, Alma, Amulek, Nephi son of Helaman,
   and Mormon, but absent from the Bible. This chapter is by far the
   longest surviving stretch of him, and Jacob introduces it as something
   his hearers have already 5:1 w10 "read" — a canonical text in their
   curriculum, not a novelty.
2. **The question it answers.** Jacob has just asked how the Jews, after
   rejecting Jacob 4:15 w33–37 "they will reject the stone", can
   Jacob 4:17 w18–21 "ever build upon it". Rather than answer in
   his own voice, he reads an entire prophet — the allegory *is* the
   answer, and chapter 6 says so explicitly.
3. **Real olive-growing, strained.** The allegory's operations — grafting,
   pruning, digging about, dunging — are genuine Mediterranean olive
   culture; its departures (wild scions sustaining a tame root, whole
   trees transplanted) strain the craft on purpose. The vineyard behaves
   the way covenant history behaves, not the way orchards do.
4. **Servants and the long day.** The labor force changes across the
   allegory: one servant through the first three visits, then
   5:61 w5–6 "call servants" and 5:70 w29–30 "other servants" — few — for the
   final pruning. The staffing is part of the prophecy's calendar: the
   last work is a small crew working 5:62 w23–26 "for the last time".

## DEPTH: WORD LEVEL

### "cumber" (vv. 9, 30, 44, 49, 66)
To weigh down, encumber, occupy ground uselessly — five occurrences, all
in this chapter. The word tracks the allegory's economy of space: burned
branches must not 5:9 w38–43 "cumber the ground of my vineyard", and at the
end the whole vineyard is threatened as ground cumbered (v. 49). Its one
Gospel cousin is the barren fig tree that "cumbereth" the ground — a
parable that also ends with a servant pleading for one more season.

### "nethermost" (vv. 13, 14, 19, 38, 39, 52)
Lowest, farthest down — six occurrences, all in this chapter and nowhere
else in the volume. The word is the allegory's geography of scattering:
branches hidden in the 5:14 w27–31 "nethermost parts of the vineyard" are
Israel's dispersions planted in unpromising ground, and the Lord's
5:13 w16–20 "it mattereth not unto thee" makes their placement deliberate
rather than punitive. The final gathering (v. 52) harvests exactly the
places the word marked.

### "dunged" (vv. 47, 76)
The humblest verb in the Lord of the vineyard's own defense:
5:47 w28–42 "I have digged about it, and I have pruned it, and I have dunged it".
Fertilizing appears in his self-audit and in the final promise (v. 76) —
divine care described at the level of manure. The same three operations,
in nearly the same words, form the vinedresser's plea in Luke's fig-tree
parable.

### "loftiness" (v. 48)
The servant's one diagnosis: 5:48 w13–20 "Is it not the loftiness of thy vineyard—have"
— height itself as the disease, branches outgrowing roots and
5:48 w50–53 "taking strength unto themselves". Within the volume's original
composition the word occurs only here (its one other appearance is in
quoted Isaiah), and its placement is pointed: the explanation of universal
corruption is offered not by the Lord but by the servant, and accepted.

### "graft" (throughout)
The allegory's signature operation — over a dozen occurrences here, with
its only other volume appearances in Nephi's earlier olive-tree passages
(1 Ne. 10, 15). Grafting is the allegory's whole account of salvation by position:
no branch is saved or lost by what it is, only by where it is joined and
what root feeds it. The final movement's double grafting (vv. 67–68) —
natural into natural — undoes every dispersal the verb performed.

## DEPTH: PHRASE LEVEL

### "it grieveth me that I should lose" (vv. 7, 11, 13, 32, 46, 47, 51, 66)
The Lord of the vineyard's refrain — eight soundings across the chapter,
carrying most of the volume's occurrences of the phrase. The formula
never changes and never resolves: from the first pruning
(5:7 w20–28 "It grieveth me that I should lose this tree") to the last
(5:66 w2–13 "it grieveth me that I should lose the trees of my vineyard"),
every intervention in the allegory is motivated by the same sentence.
Loss-grief, not anger, is the engine of the whole history.

### "What could I have done more?" (vv. 41, 47, 49)
Asked three times in the third visit, and answered by no one. The
question is Isaiah's — the Song of the Vineyard asks it in nearly the
same words — but Zenos relocates it from Isaiah's prelude-to-judgment
into a courtroom where judgment keeps being deferred: after the second
asking the Lord moves to burn (v. 49), and the servant's four words
(v. 50) stop him. The question's third life is in this volume's later
speakers, who quote it back into sermons.

### "the Lord of the vineyard wept" (v. 41)
The allegory's center of gravity: the owner of the vineyard weeping over
it before asking his question. A weeping God is rare enough in scripture
that the moments name each other across volumes — Jesus at Lazarus's
tomb, and Enoch's God who weeps over the residue of his children. Here
the tears belong to the *master*, mid-labor, with the burning already
proposed — grief as the interval in which mercy gets its word in.

### "Spare it a little longer" (vv. 50–51)
Four words from the servant, granted verbatim:
5:50 w12–16 "Spare it a little longer" / 5:51 w5–12 "Yea, I will spare it a little longer".
The exchange is the allegory's hinge — the only moment a subordinate's
speech changes the owner's announced course — and the grant immediately
becomes the final labor program (vv. 52–74). Intercession in this chapter
is not pleading against justice; it is buying time for work.

### "the end draweth nigh" (vv. 62, 64)
The last-labor refrain, twice, bracketing the digging and dunging of the
final visit: 5:62 w14–19 "for behold the end draweth nigh". Urgency in the
allegory is a grower's — a season closing — and both soundings attach
to labor commands, not to warnings: the nearness of the end is the reason
to work, never merely to fear.

### "the first may be last... the last may be first" (v. 63)
The grafting order of the final gathering:
5:63 w5–20 "begin at the last that they may be first, and that the first may be last"
— the Gospel reversal-saying planted inside a work order. In Zenos's
economy the phrase is not about rank but sequence: the latest-scattered
branches are the first re-grafted, which the volume elsewhere reads as
Gentile-then-Israel ordering of the last days.

## DEPTH: VERSE LEVEL

**(vv. 1–3)** Jacob's frame and the likening. The chapter opens as a
citation — 5:1 w11–16 "the words of the prophet Zenos" — and the allegory
opens as a simile: 5:3 w7–20 "I will liken thee, O house of Israel, like unto a tame olive tree",
already 5:3 w34–35 "waxed old" and beginning 5:3 w38–39 "to decay". One verse in,
the terms are set: Israel is a cultivated thing, aging, in an owned
vineyard.

**(vv. 4–14)** First visit: decay and the two-way graft. The master
prunes, digs, nourishes (vv. 4–5); the response is partial —
5:6 w18–21 "young and tender branches", but a perishing
5:6 w25–26 "main top". The double operation follows: wild branches grafted
into the tame tree (vv. 7–10), natural branches hidden
5:13 w6–12 "in the nethermost part of my vineyard" (vv. 8, 13–14). The
first grieving-formula sounds (v. 7), the first burning is decreed for
withered branches (v. 7), and the visit ends with the vineyard's
population scattered by design.

**(vv. 15–28)** Second visit: good fruit everywhere. A
5:15 w8–11 "long time passed away", and the tour finds the wild-grafted
mother tree 5:17 w33–36 "begun to bear fruit" — the wild branches bringing
forth 5:18 w49–50 "tame fruit", which the Lord credits to
5:18 w36–41 "the much strength of the root". The nethermost inspection
finds the hidden branches fruitful in poor ground (vv. 20–22, the
5:22 w19–22 "poor spot of ground" exchange and its rebuke —
5:22 w10–12 "Counsel me not"), a poorer spot still fruitful (v. 23), and
5:25 w11–12 "the last", planted in 5:25 w19–23 "a good spot of ground", divided:
5:25 w33–55 "only a part of the tree hath brought forth tame fruit, and the other part of the tree hath brought forth wild fruit".
The servant's first intercession (v. 27 — 5:27 w17–21 "nourish it a little longer")
already rehearses v. 50. The visit closes on shared labor (v. 28).

**(vv. 29–49)** Third visit: universal corruption and the weeping. Another
long time, and everything is inverted — the mother tree's
5:32 w24–28 "all kinds of bad fruit", the servant's diagnosis that the wild
branches 5:34 w22–25 "have nourished the roots" but overrun them (v. 37), and
the nethermost trees corrupted with them (vv. 38–40), the choicest spot
worst of all (5:43 w24–36 "choice unto me above all other parts of the land of my vineyard").
Then the chapter's summit: the weeping and the question (v. 41), the
Lord's self-audit (vv. 42, 47 — nourished, digged, pruned, dunged,
5:47 w46–54 "stretched forth mine hand almost all the day long"), the
servant's loftiness diagnosis (v. 48), the burning proposed (v. 49), and
the four-word stay (v. 50, treated at phrase level).

**(vv. 50–74)** The last labor. The grant (v. 51) becomes a program:
cross-grafting the scattered branches home (vv. 52–56, natural
5:56 w15–18 "into their mother tree"), gradual clearing rather than
clear-cutting — 5:57 w11–26 "Pluck not the wild branches from the trees, save it be those which are most bitter",
5:65 w7–17 "ye shall clear away the branches which bring forth bitter fruit"
as the good grows, 5:66 w19–26 "the bad according as the good shall grow" —
new servants called and few (vv. 61, 70), the reversal ordering (v. 63),
equal labor (5:72 w17–25 "the Lord of the vineyard labored also with them"),
and the result: 5:73 w6–12 "the natural fruit again in the vineyard", the
bad cast away, until the vineyard stands
5:74 w49–57 "like unto one body; and the fruits were equal".

**(vv. 75–77)** The season and the end. The Lord blesses the last
laborers — 5:75 w81–85 "because ye have been diligent" — promises a long
laying-up 5:76 w21–26 "against the season, which speedily cometh", and
schedules the true end: when evil fruit returns, the good gathered, the
bad 5:77 w40–45 "cast away into its own place", and the vineyard
5:77 w62–64 "burned with fire". The allegory refuses a sentimental close —
the spared vineyard is spared *for a season*, and the last verse hands
the burning back.

## DEPTH: CHAPTER LEVEL

1. **Structure:** the allegory keeps time by visits — four tours of the
   vineyard (vv. 4–14, 15–28, 29–49, 50–74) separated by
   5:29 w7–12 "a long time had passed away" formulas, with a frame (vv. 1–3)
   and an epilogue (vv. 75–77). Each visit has the same skeleton — arrive,
   inspect, converse, act — which lets the differences carry the meaning:
   what changes visit to visit is the fruit, the diagnosis, and how close
   the fire comes.
2. **A dialogue, not a description.** Nearly everything happens in speech
   between the Lord and the servant: proposals, objections
   (5:21 w13–16 "How comest thou hither"), rebukes, diagnoses, intercessions.
   The allegory's doctrine of divine-human labor is carried by its form —
   the owner explains himself to his help, accepts their counsel twice
   (vv. 27, 50), and works beside them (v. 72).
3. **The economy of grief.** Count the motives the text actually states:
   grief at loss (eight times), preservation
   (5:53 w14–21 "I may preserve unto myself the roots thereof"), and joy in
   fruit. Wrath never appears; even the burnings are ground-management
   (5:9 w34–43 "that they may not cumber the ground of my vineyard"). The
   allegory's judgment scenes run entirely on a grower's necessity
   plus sorrow — a account of divine severity with no anger in it.
4. **Strained craft as argument.** The allegory bends real olive-growing
   exactly where the doctrine needs it: wild scions should not be
   sustained by a tame root (vv. 17–18 has the Lord credit the root
   anyway), and no orchardist grafts to *save the root* rather than the
   fruit — but that inversion (vv. 34–36, 53) is the point: the covenant
   root outranks any generation's branches.

## DEPTH: BOOK LEVEL

1. **The center of Jacob's book.** The book of Jacob is built around this
   quotation: chapter 4 poses the question (the rejected stone), chapter 5
   reads Zenos, chapter 6 applies him —
   Jacob 6:1 w20–26 "the things which this prophet Zenos spake"
   — and pivots to Sherem, whose flat denial of Christ (ch. 7) plays the
   loftiness the allegory diagnosed. Structurally, Jacob answers his
   hardest question by yielding the floor for seventy-five verses.
2. **The family's olive-tree inheritance.** Lehi taught the scattering
   and grafting of Israel as an olive tree (1 Ne. 10), and Nephi expounded
   the grafting to his brothers (1 Ne. 15); Jacob supplies the source text
   the family tradition was compressing. Within the small plates the
   allegory is the long-form original of which the earlier mentions now
   read as summaries.
3. **The servant's four words and the book's priests.** Jacob and his
   priestly line hold 5:50 w12–16 "Spare it a little longer" as their job
   description — the book opened with Jacob magnifying an office of
   warning (Jacob 1:19), and the allegory locates that office inside
   God's own deliberations: the vineyard's history bends at the point
   where a servant speaks.
4. **A patience text in an anxious book.** Jacob's book is otherwise
   short-breathed — grief at his people's pride, Sherem's challenge, the
   melancholy farewell. The allegory is its long exhale: a history in
   which corruption is total *and* the owner keeps returning, for
   5:76 w4–6 "a long time", against the season. The book's famous sorrow
   (7:26) reads differently after seventy-seven verses of a Lord who
   grieves and keeps working.

## WORLD IN FRONT OF THE TEXT

### Applications

**Working the interval (vv. 50–74).** The allegory's mercy is not a
pardon but a labor window — "spare it" immediately becomes dig, dung,
graft, clear away gradually. Applied to any relationship or community I
have privately consigned to the fire: the faithful move is not a verdict
but another season of specific, patient operations, cleared
5:66 w21–26 "according as the good shall grow".

**Letting the root outrank the branch (vv. 34–37, 53).** The Lord keeps
choosing the root — the covenant, the long line — over any single
generation's branches, including good ones. The application runs against
instinct: in family and faith, tend what feeds the generations rather
than defending the current branch's appearance.

### Self-reflection questions

- (v. 41) When I ask "what more could I have done," am I asking it the
  Lord of the vineyard's way — mid-labor, in grief, with tools still in
  hand — or as a closing argument?
- (vv. 21–22) What "poor spot of ground" am I second-guessing that may be
  exactly where nourishment has been counseled?
- (v. 47) Which of the four verbs — dig, prune, dung, nourish — is
  missing from my care of the person or work I claim grieves me?
