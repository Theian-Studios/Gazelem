## Compassion and Love
His most frequent recorded feeling — and the love that persists even in refusal.

- Compassion | “Moved with compassion” // Toward the multitudes, “as sheep having no shepherd”; the leper; the widow of Nain | σπλαγχνίζομαι · *splanchnizomai* — “to be moved with compassion” // *in the text:* ἐσπλαγχνίσθη *(esplanchnisthē)* aorist passive — “he was moved with compassion” | Matt 9:36 (14:14; Mk 1:41; Lk 7:13)
- Love (a single act) | “Jesus beholding him loved him” // The rich young ruler — loved at the very moment of the hard call | ἀγαπάω · *agapaō* — “to love” // *in the text:* ἠγάπησεν *(ēgapēsen)* aorist active — “he loved — then and there” | Mark 10:21
- Love (ongoing) | “Now Jesus loved Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus” // The household at Bethany; and “the disciple whom Jesus loved” | ἀγαπάω · *agapaō* — “to love” // *in the text:* ἠγάπα *(ēgapa)* imperfect active — “he kept on loving” | John 11:5 (13:23)

## Grief, Weeping, and Groaning
Two weepings, two different Greek verbs — a distinction most English readers never see.

- Wept (silent tears) | “Jesus wept” // At the tomb of Lazarus, before He raised him | δακρύω · *dakryō* — “to shed tears” // *in the text:* ἐδάκρυσεν *(edakrysen)* aorist active — “he shed tears — the only time this verb appears in the NT” | John 11:35
- Wept (aloud) | “He beheld the city, and wept over it” // Over Jerusalem at the triumphal entry | κλαίω · *klaiō* — “to wail, weep aloud” // *in the text:* ἔκλαυσεν *(eklausen)* aorist active — “he wailed, wept openly” | Luke 19:41
- Groaned / indignant | “He groaned in the spirit, and was troubled” // At Lazarus’ tomb, seeing Mary and the Jews weeping | ἐμβριμάομαι · *embrimaomai* — “to snort; to feel stern, indignant emotion” // *in the text:* ἐνεβριμήσατο *(enebrimēsato)* aorist middle — “he was deeply moved — with an edge of anger” | John 11:33, 38
- Anger with grief | “Looked round about on them with anger, being grieved for the hardness of their hearts” // In the synagogue, before healing the withered hand | ὀργή + συλλυπέομαι · *orgē + syllypeōmai* — “anger + to be grieved together” // *in the text:* μετ’ ὀργῆς ... συλλυπούμενος *(met’ orgēs ... syllypoumenos)* noun (gen.) + pres. mid. participle — “with anger... being grieved” | Mark 3:5
- Sighing | “He sighed / he sighed deeply in his spirit” // At the deaf man; and at the Pharisees who sought a sign | στενάζω / ἀναστενάζω · *stenazō / anastenazō* — “to sigh / to sigh deeply (up from the depths)” // *in the text:* ἐστέναξεν / ἀναστενάξας *(estenaxen / anastenaxas)* aorist / aorist participle — “he sighed / sighing from the depths” | Mark 7:34 (8:12)

## Anguish in the Passion
The vocabulary of Gethsemane — the heaviest words the Gospels possess.

- Troubled in spirit | “Now is my soul troubled / troubled in spirit” // Foretelling His death; and foretelling the betrayal | ταράσσω · *tarassō* — “to stir up, agitate (as wind on water)” // *in the text:* τετάρακται / ἐταράχθη *(tetaraktai / etarachthē)* perfect passive (12:27) / aorist passive (13:21) — “is troubled / was troubled” | John 12:27 (13:21)
- Sorrowful & distressed | “My soul is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death” // In Gethsemane — “he began to be sorrowful and very heavy” | περίλυπος + ἀδημονέω · *perilűpos + adēmoneō* — “encircled by grief + to be in anguish” // *in the text:* Περίλυπος ... ἀδημονεῖν *(Perilűpos ... adēmonein)* adjective + present infinitive — “exceeding sorrowful... to be distressed” | Matt 26:37–38 (Mk 14:33–34)
- Agony | “Being in an agony he prayed more earnestly” // In Gethsemane — “his sweat was as it were great drops of blood” | ἀγωνία · *agōnia* — “agony — the tension of a contest (from ἀγών, an arena)” // *in the text:* ἀγωνίᾳ *(agōnia)* dative noun — “in agony, in the struggle” | Luke 22:44

## Joy, Wonder, and Desire
He rejoiced, He longed — and He marvelled exactly twice.

- Rejoicing | “In that hour Jesus rejoiced in spirit” // When the Seventy returned; thanking the Father who reveals to babes | ἀγαλλιάω · *agalliaō* — “to exult, leap for joy” // *in the text:* ἠγαλλιάσατο *(ēgalliasato)* aorist middle — “he exulted — far stronger than ordinary rejoicing” | Luke 10:21
- Fervent desire | “With desire I have desired to eat this passover with you” // At the Last Supper | ἐπιθυμέω · *epithymeō* — “to desire, long for” // *in the text:* ἐπιθυμίᾳ ἐπεθύμησα *(epithymia epethymēsa)* cognate noun + aorist verb (a Hebraism) — “I have earnestly desired” | Luke 22:15
- Marvelled (at faith) | “He marvelled... I have not found so great faith” // At the centurion of Capernaum — a Gentile | θαυμάζω · *thaumazō* — “to marvel, wonder” // *in the text:* ἐθαύμασεν *(ethaumasen)* aorist active — “he marvelled — in an instant” | Matt 8:10 (Lk 7:9)
- Marvelled (at unbelief) | “He marvelled because of their unbelief” // At Nazareth, His own country — the mirror image | θαυμάζω · *thaumazō* — “to marvel, wonder” // *in the text:* ἐθαύμαζεν *(ethaumazen)* imperfect active — “he kept on marvelling” | Mark 6:6

## Reading the Greek: A Closer Look

**σπλαγχνίζομαι — compassion from the gut.** The verb is built on τὰ σπλάγχνα (ta splanchna), the inward parts — heart, liver, lungs — which the ancients held to be the seat of the deepest feeling. To be “moved with compassion” is literally to be moved in the viscera, a physical churning of pity. In the Gospels it is reserved almost entirely for Jesus, or for a figure who stands for God (the father of the prodigal, the Samaritan, the forgiving king), and it nearly always issues at once in action. At Matthew 9:36 the form is ἐσπλαγχνίσθη, aorist passive — the compassion seizes Him. One text worth knowing: at Mark 1:41, where the King James reads “moved with compassion,” several of the earliest manuscripts read ὀργισθείς, “moved with anger” — one of the most debated variants in the Gospels.

**ἀγαπάω — and the difference a tense makes.** The same verb stands behind two very different moments, and the tense is the whole point. At Mark 10:21 it is ἠγάπησεν, aorist — a single, decisive movement of love at the instant of the hard call the young man would not take. At John 11:5 it is ἠγάπα, imperfect — a continuous, habitual love: “Jesus kept on loving Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus.” The aorist is a snapshot; the imperfect is a reel of film. (This is the verb of the “lovest thou me” exchange in John 21, where it alternates with φιλέω — whether that shift is meaningful or stylistic is long debated.)

**δακρύω versus κλαίω — two ways to weep.** δακρύω (John 11:35) means to let tears well and fall — quiet weeping — and it occurs nowhere else in the entire New Testament. κλαίω (Luke 19:41) means to wail, to weep aloud, the word used for mourning the dead and for the women who bewailed Him on the road to the cross. So the shortest verse in the Bible gives Him silent tears at a friend’s grave, while over the city He breaks into open lamentation — the same Lord, two registers of grief.

**ἐμβριμάομαι — the groan with an edge.** Used first of horses snorting, then of persons for a forceful, often stern emotion; elsewhere in the Gospels it means to charge someone sternly (Mark 1:43; Matthew 9:30). At Lazarus’ tomb (ἐνεβριμήσατο, then the participle ἐμβριμώμενος) it suggests His grief carried indignation — not only sorrow but anger, most likely at death and unbelief themselves. The English “groaned” is too soft for it.

**ὀργή with συλλυπέομαι — anger and grief at once.** Mark 3:5 is the only place the Gospels name ὀργή, anger, as Jesus’ own feeling: “with anger” (μετ’ ὀργῆς), and in the same breath συλλυπούμενος — συν (together with) + λυπέομαι (to grieve). The two are held together: indignation at the hardness of heart, sorrow for the men who carry it.

**ταράσσω — the troubled spirit.** To stir up or agitate, as wind on water. John 12:27 uses the perfect τετάρακται — “now is my soul troubled,” a disturbance already settled upon Him; John 13:21 uses the aorist ἐταράχθη — “he was troubled in spirit” at the moment He named the betrayer. The tense tracks the difference between an abiding weight and a sudden shock.

**περίλυπος and ἀδημονέω — the words of Gethsemane.** περίλυπος is περί (around, all over) + λύπη (grief) — grief that encircles and overwhelms: “exceeding sorrowful, even unto death.” ἀδημονέω is among the strongest words for distress the language has — to be in anguish, sore troubled, at a loss. Mark adds ἐκθαμβεῖσθαι, to be struck with awe or horror. Together they are the heaviest terms the Gospels reach for.

**ἀγωνία — the wrestler’s anguish.** From ἀγών, a contest or arena — the tension of a combatant in the struggle. The English “agony” still carries it. A textual note for careful use: Luke 22:43–44, the strengthening angel and the sweat like drops of blood, are absent from several of the earliest manuscripts and are double-bracketed in critical editions.

**ἀγαλλιάω — leaping joy.** Probably from ἄγαν (much) and ἅλλομαι (to leap) — joy that leaps, exultation, well beyond ordinary χαίρω (to rejoice). Luke 10:21 is the one place we are told Jesus rejoiced, and the Greek makes it exuberant: He exulted in the Spirit.

**ἐπιθυμίᾳ ἐπεθύμησα — desire heaped on desire.** Luke 22:15 sets the noun ἐπιθυμία (desire) beside its own cognate verb ἐπεθύμησα (I desired) — a Hebrew idiom, like the infinitive absolute, that English can only carry with an adverb: “I have earnestly desired to eat this passover with you.” Not two longings, but one, intensified.

**θαυμάζω — and the two marvels.** He marvels exactly twice, and again the tense marks them. At the centurion’s faith (Matthew 8:10) it is ἐθαύμασεν, aorist — a single, instantaneous wonder. At Nazareth’s unbelief (Mark 6:6) it is ἐθαύμαζεν, imperfect — a lingering, ongoing amazement. Gentile faith astonishes Him in a moment; His own town’s unbelief astonishes Him and keeps on astonishing Him.
