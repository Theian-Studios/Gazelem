# The Instruments: Interpreters, Seer Stone, and the Meaning of "Urim and Thummim"
* image: the-instruments.jpg

## The Nephite Interpreters

When Joseph Smith received the plates from Moroni on September 22, 1827, he received with them an ancient instrument the Book of Mormon itself calls "interpreters" — two stones prepared and transmitted among Nephite seers precisely for the translation of records (Mosiah 8:13; 28:13–20; Ether 4:5). Joseph described the device in his 1838 history: "with the record was found... two stones in silver bows — and these stones, fastened to a breastplate, constituted what is called the Urim and Thummim" (Joseph Smith—History 1:35). The 1842 Wentworth Letter repeats the description: "two transparent stones set in the rim of a bow fastened to a breast plate."

Family members who handled the objects through cloth left independent descriptions. Lucy Mack Smith examined the instrument and described stones like "two smooth three-cornered diamonds set in glass, and the glasses were set in silver bows" connected in the manner of "old fashioned spectacles" (*Biographical Sketches*, 1853). Joseph's younger brother William added the practical detail that the spectacles were set too wide for Joseph's eyes, suggesting they were fitted for a larger frame — consistent with the Book of Mormon's attribution of the instrument to earlier peoples (William Smith interview, *The Saints' Herald*, 1884; cf. J. W. Peterson account). Joseph Knight Sr., present the morning the plates were obtained, recalled Joseph's delight: "he seemed to think more of the glasses or the urim and thummim than he did of the plates, for, says he, 'I can see any thing; they are marvelous'" (Joseph Knight Sr., Reminiscence, ca. 1835–47, spelling modernized).

## The Seer Stone

The documentary record is equally clear that Joseph employed a second instrument: a small, chocolate-colored seer stone he had found around 1822 while digging a well for Willard Chase (per Martin Harris, the stone was discovered some twenty-four feet underground; Harris interview, *Tiffany's Monthly*, 1859). Joseph's use of such a stone situated him within a widespread early-American folk culture of seeric practice — a context treated at length by Richard Bushman (*Rough Stone Rolling*, 2005) and by Church historians Richard E. Turley Jr., Robin S. Jensen, and Mark Ashurst-McGee in "Joseph the Seer" (*Ensign*, October 2015), the article that first published photographs of the stone, which has remained in the custody of the First Presidency since Brigham Young's era.

The witnesses closest to the translation describe the stone as the working instrument for much, likely most, of the extant text — particularly after the loss of the 116 pages in mid-1828, when the interpreters were taken from Joseph for a season. Emma Smith: "In writing for your father I frequently wrote day after day, often sitting at the table close by him, he sitting with his face buried in his hat, with the stone in it, and dictating hour after hour with nothing between us" (*Saints' Herald*, 1879). David Whitmer, in whose father's Fayette home the final portion was translated, gave the fullest description:

> "Joseph Smith would put the seer stone into a hat, and put his face in the hat, drawing it closely around his face to exclude the light; and in the darkness the spiritual light would shine. A piece of something resembling parchment would appear, and on that appeared the writing. One character at a time would appear, and under it was the interpretation in English. Brother Joseph would read off the English to Oliver Cowdery, who was his principal scribe, and when it was written down and repeated to Brother Joseph to see if it was correct, then it would disappear, and another character with the interpretation would appear. Thus the Book of Mormon was translated by the gift and power of God, and not by any power of man." (*An Address to All Believers in Christ*, Richmond, Mo., 1887, 12)

Martin Harris, through Edward Stevenson's 1881 account, described the same feedback mechanism:

> "By aid of the seer stone, sentences would appear and were read by the Prophet and written by Martin, and when finished he would say, 'Written,' and if correctly written, that sentence would disappear and another appear in its place, but if not written correctly it remained until corrected, so that the translation was just as it was engraven on the plates, precisely in the language then used." Even the hostile witness Isaac Hale corroborated the physical method while rejecting its claims: "The manner in which he pretended to read and interpret, was the same as when he looked for the money-diggers, with the stone in his hat, and his hat over his face" (affidavit, 1834, in *Mormonism Unvailed*).

## One Term, Two Instruments

The apparent tension between "spectacles" accounts and "stone in the hat" accounts dissolves on terminological grounds. The label "Urim and Thummim" — borrowed from the revelatory instrument of Israel's high priest (Exodus 28:30; Ezra 2:63) — does not appear in the earliest sources; it was applied retrospectively, first in print by W. W. Phelps in *The Evening and the Morning Star* (January 1833), and thereafter early Saints used it elastically to denote the Nephite interpreters, the seer stone, or seeric instruments generally. Wilford Woodruff, for example, called a seer stone a Urim and Thummim in 1888. The Church's Gospel Topics essay "Book of Mormon Translation" (2013) synthesizes this record and affirms both instruments, as does the Joseph Smith Papers project throughout its documentary apparatus.

## Interpretive Significance

Two scholarly observations follow. First, the mechanics described by the witnesses are evidentially potent: a translator with his face buried in a hat cannot consult a manuscript, Bible, or notes, and Emma expressly testified Joseph had "neither manuscript nor book to read from." The method that has embarrassed some modern readers functions, historically, as an argument against fraud. Second, the trajectory of instrument use — from spectacles, to stone, to (in later revelatory work) no instrument at all — traces a developmental arc. As Orson Pratt reported Joseph explaining, the Lord gave him instruments when he was inexperienced, "until he was acquainted with the Spirit of revelation" (Pratt, *Millennial Star*, 1874). The instruments were scaffolding for a seer in training; the constant, in every account, was the gift itself: "by the gift and power of God, and not by any power of man" (Whitmer, *Address*, 1887).
