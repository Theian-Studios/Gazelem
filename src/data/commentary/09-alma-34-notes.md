## WORLD BEHIND THE TEXT

**1. Date and political moment.** About 74 BC — the seventeenth year of the
reign of the judges (Alma 35:12 [BOOK] closes the year immediately after this
sermon's aftermath). Mosiah's monarchy ended in 91 BC; the judgeship is less
than a generation old, and the recurring threat to it is not foreign invasion
but internal dissent: Amlici (Alma 2), the Amalekites and Amulonites (Alma
21–24), and now the Zoramites. Alma has already resigned the chief judgeship
(Alma 4:16–20 [BOOK]) precisely because he judged the word "more powerful" than
the office — this mission is that wager in action.

**2. The mission as statecraft.** Alma 31:4 [BOOK] states the Nephite fear
plainly: the Zoramites occupied a border land and might "enter into a
correspondence with the Lamanites." The mission to Antionum is therefore
simultaneously pastoral and geopolitical — reclaiming dissenters is border
defense by other means (Alma 31:5's word-vs-sword calculus). The sermon in
Alma 34 sits inside a national-security problem, and its failure among the
ruling Zoramites produces exactly the feared alliance (Alma 35:10–11 [BOOK]).

**3. Place.** Antionum: east of the land of Zarahemla, nearly bordering the
seashore, south of the land of Jershon, with "the wilderness south... full of
the Lamanites" beyond it (Alma 31:3 [BOOK]). Jershon — one land north — matters
twice: it is where the people of Ammon (Lamanite converts) already live, and it
is where the converted Zoramite poor will flee (Alma 35:6 [BOOK]). The chapter's
audience is standing a day or two's travel from their eventual refuge.

**4. Who the Zoramites are.** Nephite dissenters "gathered... together" under a
man named Zoram (Alma 31:1, 8 [BOOK]) — religiously innovative, not lapsed.
They kept synagogues and a weekly holy day but denied Christ, rejected the law
of Moses' ongoing relevance, prayed one fixed prayer from one fixed stand
(the Rameumptom), and fused election theology with wealth display ("costly
apparel," Alma 31:28 [BOOK]). Socially they had produced a two-tier religion:
the poor were "cast out of the synagogues" they had labored to build (Alma
32:2–3, 5 [BOOK]) — expelled, in effect, for the sin of poverty. Amulek's
audience is that lower tier, gathered on the hill Onidah (Alma 32:4 [BOOK]).

**5. Religious infrastructure of the audience's world.** Nephite worship at
this period still operates the law of Moses — sacrifice and offering included
(Mosiah 2:3 [BOM]; Alma 30:3 [BOOK]) — while teaching that the law points
forward to Christ. This is what makes vv. 10–14 land: Amulek is telling people
who watch animals bleed on altars that the whole apparatus is one long gesture
toward a final sacrifice. Synagogues "built after the manner of the Jews"
(Alma 16:13 [BOOK]) are the standard venue for preaching, which is what makes
expulsion from them so religiously disenfranchising — and what gives v. 38's
"worship... in whatsoever place ye may be in" its force.

**6. The brass-plates canon.** Zenos and Zenock (v. 7) are prophets preserved
on the plates of brass but absent from our Old Testament — evidence that the
Nephite scriptural canon is wider than, and independent of, the biblical one.
Alma's use of them in ch. 33 and Amulek's appeal to them here treat them as
common ground even apostate Zoramites must credit, suggesting the brass plates
functioned as shared public scripture across Nephite factions.

**7. Amulek's biographical world.** Amulek is an Ammonihahite — a wealthy,
well-connected man (Alma 10:4 [BOOK]) from a city whose legal class ran on
litigation-for-hire (Alma 10:32 [BOOK]) and whose theology (the order of Nehor)
denied that atonement was needed at all. Seven years before this sermon, his
city refused the procrastination warning and was annihilated in a day (Alma
16:9–11 [BOOK]); his converts were burned in front of him (Alma 14 [BOOK]); his
family and friends rejected him (Alma 15:16 [BOOK]). The economy of the chapter
— legal argument, urgency about closing windows, sympathy for the dispossessed —
is his lived history speaking.

**8. Agrarian texture.** The prayer-catalog (vv. 20–25) is an inventory of the
audience's actual economy: fields, crops, flocks (twice), households with
morning/mid-day/evening rhythms. These are subsistence farmers and herders on a
frontier — people whose "welfare" (v. 27) is genuinely weather-and-harvest
dependent, which is why Amulek sanctifies temporal petitions rather than
ranking them below spiritual ones.

## DEPTH: WORD LEVEL

### "expedient" (vv. 9, 10, 13)
Archaic sense: *necessary, required by the nature of things* — not the modern
"convenient." Amulek uses it five times in five verses, hammering that the
atonement is not one option among several but a structural necessity of the
"great plan of the Eternal God." The repetition is the argument: each occurrence
narrows the claim (an atonement must be made → it must be a sacrifice → the
sacrifice must be infinite).

### "infinite" (vv. 10, 12, 14)
Set against three finite categories Amulek explicitly rules out: man, beast,
fowl (v. 10) — the full range of Mosaic offerings plus the pagan option of human
sacrifice. "Infinite" here does double duty: unbounded in *scope* (suffices "for
the sins of the world," v. 12) and unbounded in *kind* (a divine, not creaturely,
offering — "the Son of God, yea, infinite and eternal," v. 14, where the titles
attach to the person, not just the act).

### "jot... tittle" / "every whit" (vv. 13–14)
Jot = yod, the smallest Hebrew letter; tittle = a small distinguishing stroke.
Amulek: the law is fulfilled down to its smallest orthographic unit, and "every
whit" of it points to the great and last sacrifice — i.e., the law is not merely
obeyed to completion but *read* to completion, as one long type of Christ.

### "cry" (vv. 18–27)
Nine imperative uses in ten verses. "Cry" in the KJV register is not casual
asking but distressed calling-out (as in the Israelites "crying" in bondage).
Amulek assigns it to fields, flocks, houses, households, enemies, the devil,
crops — the entire economy of a dispossessed agrarian audience — then pivots to
the silent version: hearts "drawn out in prayer... continually" (v. 27).

### "closets" (v. 26)
Private inner rooms. The triad "closets... secret places... wilderness" moves
prayer from public devotion (vv. 18–25) to concealment — worship no synagogue
can gatekeep. Pointed for Zoramites barred from their own synagogues (Alma 32:2–3).

### "dross" (v. 29)
Refiner's waste — the slag skimmed off molten metal and discarded. The sting:
in Alma 32:3 the poor Zoramites "were esteemed by their brethren as dross."
Amulek warns them not to *become* spiritually what others wrongly esteemed them
socially — the uncharitable, whatever their poverty, are the true castoff.

### "improve" (v. 33)
Archaic: *to make profitable use of, to turn to account* (as in "improving" land
or time) — not "to make better." "If we do not improve our time while in this
life" = if we do not put the probationary day to its intended use. Reads as a
labor metaphor continuous with "the day... to perform their labors" (v. 32).

### "seal" (v. 35)
Ownership-mark: to seal is to stamp as one's property. He who dies having
procrastinated repentance is "sealed" the devil's — the dark mirror of being
sealed the Lord's. Note the mechanism is withdrawal ("the Spirit of the Lord
hath withdrawn"), not seizure; the devil's power fills a vacancy.

## DEPTH: PHRASE LEVEL

### "great and last sacrifice" (vv. 10, 13, 14 ×2, 15)
Five occurrences. "Great" answers the scale of the fall (all "hardened...
fallen... lost," v. 9); "last" is the end-of-history claim — this sacrifice
terminates the entire sacrificial system ("a stop to the shedding of blood,"
v. 13). Amulek is announcing the end of the law of Moses *from within* its
observance, decades before the fact.

### "faith unto repentance" (vv. 15, 16 ×2, 17)
Four occurrences in three verses — and the phrase appears **nowhere else in
scripture**. It is Amulek's own coinage and his precise doctrine of salvation: faith is
not the terminus but the engine, and repentance is what faith is *for*. The
atonement "bringeth about means" (v. 15) — it creates the conditions under which
faith can operate — but mercy's claim is activated only in "him that has faith
unto repentance" (v. 16). This is the chapter's answer to the Zoramite creed of
unconditional election (Alma 31:16–17).

### "bowels of mercy, which overpowereth justice" (v. 15)
"Bowels" renders the Hebrew idiom (raḥamim) for visceral compassion — mercy felt
in the gut. "Overpowereth" is startling and deliberately provisional: v. 16
immediately corrects the frame to "mercy can *satisfy* the demands of justice."
Read as a two-step: experientially mercy overwhelms; structurally it satisfies —
justice is paid, not defeated. Amulek gives in one breath what Alma 42 will take
a chapter to unfold.

### "encircles them in the arms of safety" (v. 16)
The Book of Mormon runs a sustained *encirclement* polarity: one is encircled
either by chains or by arms. Amulek's image is the redemptive pole.

### "mighty to save" (v. 18)
Isaiah 63:1 — the divine warrior "travelling in the greatness of his strength...
mighty to save." A one-line credal warrant for the nine "cry" imperatives that
follow: prayer is worth commanding because of who is listening.

### "trodden under foot of men" (v. 29)
Verbatim from the salt saying (Matt. 5:13): salt that loses its savor is "cast
out, and... trodden under foot of men." Amulek splices the salt saying's fate
onto the dross image — two figures of worthless residue fused into one warning
about charity-less religion.

---

### "prepare to meet God" (v. 32)
A near-verbatim lift of Amos 4:12 ("prepare to meet thy God, O Israel") —
in Amos a threat, in Amulek an invitation with a deadline. The relocation of the
prophetic warning-formula into a doctrine of probation is the move that makes
vv. 32–35 the Restoration's classic text on mortality-as-preparation.

### "the night of darkness wherein there can be no labor" (v. 33)
The day-labor/night-rest frame of v. 32 turned ominous: death ends the working
day. The image is the same as Jesus' in John 9:4 ("the night cometh, when no man
can work") — one of the tightest BoM/NT phrase parallels in the chapter.

### "that same spirit... will have power to possess your body" (v. 34)
Against deathbed repentance: death changes venue, not character. "Spirit" is
deliberately ambiguous — your own disposition, and the devil's spirit to which
the procrastinator has "become subjected" (v. 35). Continuity, not judgment, is
the threat: you keep what you are.

### "garments... made white through the blood of the Lamb" (v. 36)
The paradox-image of Rev. 7:14 (washed white *in blood*). Amulek attaches it to
the indwelling clause — God "dwelleth... in the hearts of the righteous" — so
the whitened garment marks who can be a temple.

### "work out your salvation with fear before God" (v. 37)
Philippians 2:12 nearly verbatim ("work out your own salvation with fear and
trembling"). In context the phrase is Amulek's synthesis: salvation is by the
infinite atonement (vv. 8–16) *and* is worked out — the "labors" of v. 32 — with
"fear" as reverent seriousness, not terror.

### "in spirit and in truth" (v. 38)
John 4:24 verbatim — and in the same argumentative position: Jesus tells the
Samaritan woman worship is not bound to a mountain or temple; Amulek tells
synagogue-expelled Zoramites to worship "in whatsoever place ye may be in."
Both texts detach worship from contested sacred architecture.

## DEPTH: VERSE LEVEL

**(v. 1)** Staging: Alma "sat down upon the ground" — the posture of a completed
witness — and Amulek "arose." The chapter break conceals that 33 and 34 are one
continuous meeting; nothing intervenes. Amulek's entire discourse presupposes
Alma's and repeatedly points back at it ("my brother," vv. 3, 6, 7, 8).

**(vv. 2–6)** Amulek opens forensically: the Zoramites are apostates, not
novices ("taught unto you bountifully before your dissension," v. 2), so their
"great question" — whether the word is in the Son of God "or whether there shall
be no Christ" (v. 5) — is not honest ignorance but a live crisis Alma's sermon
has forced. Note that Amulek reframes Alma 32's abstract "seed/word" as
explicitly Christological: the experiment was always about Christ.

**(vv. 7–8)** The witness-stack: Zenos, Zenock, Moses (Alma's three citations in
ch. 33), and now "I will testify unto you of myself." Amulek adds a fourth,
living witness to three textual ones — the law of witnesses enacted in the
sermon's very structure.

**(v. 9)** The problem-statement: "all are hardened; yea, all are fallen and are
lost, and must perish" — universal ruin as the premise that makes the atonement
"expedient." The verse is the chapter's hinge from testimony to argument.

**(v. 10)** The definition by exclusion: not man, not beast, not fowl, not human sacrifice —
the sacrifice is defined first by what it cannot be. "Infinite and eternal"
enters here as the only remaining category.

**(vv. 11–12)** The legal argument, without close parallel in scripture: no man's blood can
atone for another's sins, *proved from Nephite homicide law* — the law takes the
murderer's own life, never a substitute's ("will our law... take the life of his
brother? I say unto you, Nay"). Human substitution being legally incoherent,
only an infinite (non-merely-human) offering can suffice. Amulek argues from the
courtroom he knows — he was a litigant against lawyers in Ammonihah.

**(vv. 13–14)** The claim about how to read the law: its fulfillment is also its
*meaning* — "every whit pointing to that great and last sacrifice." The law of
Moses is recast as one long ordinance-shaped prophecy whose referent is the Son
of God.

**(vv. 15–16)** The doctrinal core (see phrase notes on "faith unto
repentance," "bowels of mercy," "arms of safety"). Structure: intent of the
sacrifice → mercy's relation to justice → the single condition. V. 16's closing
clause is a warning wrapped in a promise: he who exercises no faith unto
repentance "is exposed to the whole law of the demands of justice" — the
atonement shields no one unconditionally.

**(v. 17)** Pivot from doctrine to devotion: "therefore" converts vv. 9–16 into
an imperative — *begin* ("that ye may begin to exercise your faith... that ye
begin to call upon his holy name"). The double "begin" respects where the
audience actually is.

**(vv. 18–25)** The cry-catalog: eight imperatives sweeping every location and
concern of an agrarian household — fields, flocks, houses, household, enemies,
the devil, crops, herds — morning, mid-day, and evening (v. 21). Two effects:
prayer is de-institutionalized (no synagogue needed) and de-compartmentalized
(no topic too temporal). See word note on "cry" for the Zenos template.

**(v. 26)** "But this is not all" — the first of two escalations. Public,
vocal, located prayer must be doubled by secret prayer: closets, secret places,
wilderness.

**(v. 27)** The interiorization: when not vocally praying, "let your hearts be
full, drawn out in prayer... continually" — prayer as a standing orientation
rather than an event, and explicitly *for others'* welfare, which plants the
hook for v. 28.

**(v. 28)** The second "do not suppose that this is all": prayer without charity
is "vain, and availeth you nothing." The tests are concrete — the needy, the
naked, the sick and afflicted, imparting substance "if ye have" (a merciful
clause for a destitute audience). Religion that stops at devotion is exposed as
hypocrisy.

**(v. 29)** See word note "dross" and phrase note "trodden under foot." The
verse fuses two residue-images into the chapter's harshest sentence — aimed not
at the rich Zoramites but at the poor, who are as capable of charity-failure as
their oppressors.

**(vv. 30–31)** "Bring fruit unto repentance" closes the loop on Alma 32's
growing cycle: seed → tree → fruit; the experiment's endpoint is repentance, not
mere conviction. "Now is the time and the day of your salvation" with
"immediately shall the great plan of redemption be brought about unto you" —
the plan's benefits are indexed to the present tense.

**(v. 32)** The probation thesis in a single sentence — likely the most-cited
verse in the chapter. See phrase note "prepare to meet God."

**(v. 33)** "Do not procrastinate the day of your repentance" — repeating
Alma's own phrase from Ammonihah verbatim (Alma 13:27), a city whose window
closed (Alma 16:2–3, 9–11). Amulek, the Ammonihahite survivor, has personal
standing to issue this warning. See phrase note on "night of darkness."

**(v. 34)** See phrase note "that same spirit." The "awful crisis" is death
faced unprepared; the argument is about human nature, not merely legal — repentance
delayed is character unformed.

**(v. 35)** The end-state of procrastination: subjection, sealing, withdrawal
of the Spirit, "the final state of the wicked." Note the agency-inversion: the
procrastinator's passivity is itself the choice that transfers power.

**(v. 36)** The warrant ("this I know, because the Lord hath said"): God dwells
not in unholy temples but "in the hearts of the righteous" — hearts, not
buildings, are the sanctuary at issue, which is the whole Zoramite problem in
one clause. Then the promised end-state pair to v. 35: the righteous "sit down
in his kingdom, to go no more out."

**(v. 37)** Summary imperative triplet: remember, work out your salvation with
fear (see phrase note), no more deny the coming of Christ — doctrine (vv. 8–16),
labor (vv. 17–33), and confession folded into one closing charge.

**(v. 38)** Four participles of discipleship: receive the Holy Ghost, take upon
you the name of Christ, worship anywhere "in spirit and in truth" (see phrase
note), live in thanksgiving daily. The verse quietly outlines a covenant order
for people with no access to religious institutions.

**(v. 39)** Watchfulness clause: prayer as the standing defense against
temptation, with the devil's economics stated flatly — "he rewardeth you no good
thing."

**(vv. 40–41)** The closing turn to the audience's actual wound: do not revile
your revilers, "lest ye become sinners like unto them"; bear afflictions "with a
firm hope that ye shall one day rest." Amulek ends not with atonement theory but
with pastoral counsel for the humiliated — patience as the poor's form of
discipleship. The "rest" promised here is the word the chapter has earned.

## DEPTH: CHAPTER LEVEL

**1. Architecture.** The discourse runs in four movements: *Witness* (vv. 1–8:
Amulek joins the witness-stack), *Atonement* (vv. 9–16: the necessity argument),
*Devotion* (vv. 17–29: prayer, escalated twice — "this is not all" — into secret
prayer and then charity), *Deadline* (vv. 30–41: probation, procrastination, and
patient endurance). Each movement ends by handing its conclusion to the next as
a premise: the atonement creates "means... unto faith unto repentance," which
demands calling on God, which is vain without charity, which must be done *now*.

**2. A two-witness sermon.** Alma 32–33 and Alma 34 are one sermon in two
voices. Alma supplies the way of coming to know and scriptural
authority (Zenos, Zenock, Moses); Amulek supplies personal testimony, doctrinal
argument, and application. The pairing itself dramatizes the law of witnesses
(v. 8; 2 Ne. 11:3 [BOM]) — the form of the meeting is part of its message.

**3. Rhetoric shaped to the audience.** Every major image is drawn from the
hearers' world: fields, flocks, crops, households (vv. 20–25) for farmers;
dross (v. 29) quoting the insult used against them (Alma 32:3 [BOOK]); law-court
reasoning (vv. 11–12) from a man who fought lawyers; worship "in whatsoever
place" (v. 38) for the synagogue-expelled. The chapter is a case study in
doctrine translated into an audience's vocabulary without dilution.

**4. The only extended atonement discourse by a "layman."** Amulek is not a
prophet, judge, or church officer — a once-wealthy convert of two years serving
as a companion. That the Book of Mormon's most-quoted necessity-of-atonement
argument comes from him is itself a claim about testimony: v. 8's "I will
testify unto you of myself" carries the sermon's weight.

**5. Anti-Zoramite point by point.** The chapter systematically negates the
Rameumptom creed (Alma 31:15–18 [BOOK]): they denied Christ → vv. 5–8 testify of
him; they held themselves elected regardless of conduct → "faith unto
repentance" makes salvation conditional; they prayed once weekly, in one spot,
in one posture → vv. 18–27 command prayer everywhere, always; they cast out the
poor → vv. 28–29 make care for the poor the test of prayer itself.

**6. Present-tense urgency as doctrine.** "Now is the time and the day" (v. 31),
"this life is the time" (v. 32), "immediately shall the great plan... be brought
about" (v. 31): the chapter's temporal claims are not rhetorical decoration but
its theology of probation — grace is available in the present tense and only
there, because character (vv. 34–35) is formed in time.

## DEPTH: BOOK LEVEL

**1. Hinge of the book's structure.** Alma divides roughly into judgment-seat
and mission narratives (1–16), the sons-of-Mosiah mission (17–27), the Zoramite
mission (31–35), Alma's testament to his sons (36–42), and the war chapters
(43–63). Alma 34 is the doctrinal summit of the Zoramite block and the last
public sermon in the book — everything after it is either private counsel or
war. Its themes (justice/mercy, probation, procrastination) are precisely the
ones Alma then develops privately to Corianton in 39–42, so chapters 34 and
40–42 function as public/private drafts of one theology.

**2. Amulek's arc.** Alma 10: a rich, connected Ammonihahite ("no small
reputation... many kindreds and friends... much riches") converted by an angelic
command. Alma 14: forced to watch the believers burn, imprisoned. Alma 15:16:
"rejected by those who were once his friends and also by his father and his
kindred" — he lost exactly what the Zoramite poor lost. Alma 34 is the payoff of
that arc: the dispossessed preaching to the dispossessed. His procrastination
warning (v. 33) quotes Alma 13:27, preached in his own doomed city (Alma 16:9–11)
— he is the surviving witness of what deferred repentance cost Ammonihah.

**3. Completion of the seed experiment.** Alma 32 plants (faith as experiment on
the word), Alma 33 names the seed (the word is Christ, per Zenos/Zenock/Moses),
Alma 34 harvests ("bring fruit unto repentance," v. 30) and prices the harvest
(the atonement that makes repentance efficacious). Read as one unit, 32–34 moves
how we know → how to read → how we are saved, and 34 is unintelligible without the
other two.

**4. The justice/mercy through-line.** Alma 12:32–35 (Ammonihah) states the
problem; Alma 34:15–16 compresses the solution; Alma 42 (to Corianton) gives the
full architecture ("justice exerciseth all his demands, and also mercy claimeth
all which is her own," 42:24). Amulek's "mercy can satisfy the demands of
justice" is the book's mid-point formulation — bolder than Alma 12, tighter than
Alma 42.

**5. The encirclement motif across Alma.** Chains (12:6; 13:30) → arms of safety
(34:16) → chains of death broken in Alma's own memory (36:18). Alma 34 supplies
the positive pole the book's imagery had lacked.

**6. Narrative consequences.** The sermon works — and its success starts a war.
Alma 35: the converted poor are expelled, received by the people of Ammon in
Jershon; the Zoramites ally with the Lamanites; the war chapters follow directly.
Alma 34's closing counsel of patience under expulsion (vv. 40–41) reads as
deliberate preparation for Alma 35's events, and the whole sequence dramatizes a
recurring Alma-book thesis: preaching the word has geopolitical consequences
(cf. Alma 31:5 — the word "more powerful... than the sword").

## WORLD IN FRONT OF THE TEXT

### Applications

**Prayer as orientation, not event (vv. 18–27).** The catalog's real claim is
that no place, hour, or topic is beneath prayer. A modern reading of vv. 20–25
would name commutes, workplaces, budgets, and inboxes where Amulek names fields
and flocks — then follow him past the vocal list to v. 27's standing posture: a
heart "drawn out... continually," including for other people's welfare, not
only one's own.

**Charity as the audit of devotion (vv. 28–29).** Amulek attaches a test to
prayer that prayer cannot pass alone: the needy, the naked, the sick. The
uncomfortable design of the verse is that it lets no one off — it is addressed
to the poor, with only the clause "if ye have" as accommodation. The application
is an audit question, not a program: does my religious practice terminate in
anyone else's relief?

**Worship without institutional access (vv. 26, 38).** The chapter was preached
to people locked out of their own religious buildings. Its counsel — closets,
secret places, wilderness, "whatsoever place ye may be in" — speaks directly to
anyone whose access to formal worship is broken: by distance, health, estrangement,
or exclusion. Standing is not gatekept by architecture.

**Refusing the labels of contempt (v. 29 with Alma 32:3 [BOOK]).** The poor were
called dross; Amulek warns them not to become it — by uncharity, the one failure
that would make the insult true. The transferable principle: mistreatment does
not license becoming what the mistreatment alleges, and v. 40 makes it explicit
("do not revile... lest ye become sinners like unto them").

**Procrastination and the shape of character (vv. 32–35).** The argument is not
that God punishes lateness but that delay *forms* the person ("that same
spirit"). Applied: the habits I am deferring change are not waiting neutrally —
they are compounding. The realistic entry point is v. 17's doubled "begin."

**Urgency without despair (vv. 31, 41).** The chapter holds two tenses at once:
"immediately shall the great plan of redemption be brought about" for the
penitent now, and "one day rest" for the afflicted later. Application: the
gospel's urgency runs toward repentance, not anxiety — and its patience runs
toward affliction, not procrastination. Reversing those (patient about
repentance, anxious under affliction) inverts the sermon.

**Bearing what cannot yet be fixed (vv. 40–41).** Amulek does not tell the poor
their expulsion will be reversed; it won't be (Alma 35:6 [BOOK]). He tells them
how to be wronged without being ruined by it. For circumstances that cannot be
solved — only survived — the counsel is patience "with a firm hope," which the
narrative then honors: they are received and nourished in Jershon.

### Self-reflection questions

- (v. 5) What is the "great question" actually in my mind right now — the one my
  reading and praying keep circling? Have I let it be asked out loud?
- (vv. 8–9) Amulek moves from "these things are true" to "I do know." Which of
  my beliefs are still inherited testimony, and what would it take to make one
  of them mine the way v. 8 is his?
- (vv. 11–12) Amulek argues from the world he knew — the courtroom. From what
  vocabulary of my own life (my work, my losses) could I bear witness in a way
  no one else can?
- (vv. 15–17) "Faith unto repentance": what is my faith currently *unto*? If I
  traced my beliefs to the behavior they actually produce, where would the
  trail end?
- (vv. 20–25) Rewrite the cry-catalog for my own economy: what are my fields,
  flocks, and enemies? Which of them have I assumed were too temporal to pray
  over?
- (v. 27) When I am not praying, what is my heart "drawn out" toward by default?
- (v. 28) Who are the needy, naked, sick, and afflicted actually within my
  reach — by name? Which "if ye have" resources am I discounting?
- (v. 29) Is there a label of contempt — from others or from myself — that I am
  in danger of growing into rather than out of?
- (vv. 31–33) What is the specific repentance I have filed under "eventually"?
  What would v. 17's "begin" look like this week, at the smallest honest scale?
- (v. 34) If "that same spirit" I carry now persisted unchanged, what would I
  be bringing with me? What would I be glad persists?
- (v. 36) What would have to change for my "heart" to be a place where God
  could comfortably dwell — and is anything currently subletting that space?
- (v. 38) Where do I *not* currently worship — places or contexts I have walled
  off from "in spirit and in truth"? What does thanksgiving "daily" look like,
  concretely, tomorrow morning?
- (vv. 40–41) Who has cast me out, in whatever sense — and am I closer to
  reviling or to the "firm hope" Amulek prescribes? What affliction am I being
  asked to bear with patience rather than resolve by force?

Each entry pins the connection to an exact word span of the Alma 34 verse:
`34:V wN–M "quoted words"` means words N through M (inclusive) of verse V,
counting whitespace-separated words from the first word after the verse number.
The quoted string is the exact text occupying that span (final punctuation
attaches to its word). Single-word anchors use `wN`.

Each entry also carries one or more `target:` lines quoting the related

initial generation, never renumbered afterward. Entries whose targets are
inside the Book of Mormon carry a `mirror:` line naming the counterpart
