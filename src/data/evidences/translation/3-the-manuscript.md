# The Manuscript: Physical Evidence of a Dictated Text
* image: the-manuscript.jpg

## Two Manuscripts and Their Fate

Textual scholarship on the Book of Mormon rests on two documents. The **original manuscript (O)** was written by scribes — chiefly Oliver Cowdery, with passages in the hands of John Whitmer, Christian Whitmer, and at least one unidentified scribe (possibly Emma Smith) — as Joseph dictated between 1828 and 1829. In October 1841 Joseph deposited it in the cornerstone of the Nauvoo House; when Lewis Bidamon opened the cornerstone in 1882, water had destroyed most of it. Approximately **28 percent survives**, the bulk held by the Church History Library. The **printer's manuscript (P)** is the complete scribal copy (lacking only about three lines) that Oliver Cowdery and two other scribes produced for typesetter John H. Gilbert of E. B. Grandin's Palmyra shop. Preserved by David Whitmer and his heirs and then by the Reorganized Church/Community of Christ, it was purchased by the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in September 2017 for $35 million — reportedly the highest price ever paid for a manuscript to that date — and is published in full-color facsimile in the Joseph Smith Papers, Revelations and Translations series, volume 3.

The definitive analysis of both documents is Royal Skousen's **Book of Mormon Critical Text Project** (1988–present), including typographical facsimiles of O and P, the six-volume *Analysis of Textual Variants*, and *The Book of Mormon: The Earliest Text* (Yale University Press, 2009).

## Evidence of Dictation

Skousen's central finding is that the original manuscript bears the physical signature of an orally dictated text rather than a visually copied or authorially composed one. Several classes of evidence converge:

**Errors of the ear, not the eye.** O contains mistakes explicable only as mishearings — homophones and near-homophones confused by a listening scribe. A frequently cited example: the scribe initially wrote "an" for "&" and confused "him"/"them" and "weed"/"reed"-type pairs in ways a copyist looking at a source text would not. By contrast, the printer's manuscript, which *was* visually copied from O, exhibits classic copying errors (eye-skips, dropped lines) — a control case confirming the distinction.

**Proper names spelled out.** Emma Smith recalled that when Joseph "came to proper names he could not pronounce, or long words, he spelled them out" (Emma Smith, interview with Edmund C. Briggs, 1856, published in *Journal of History* 9, 1916). The manuscript confirms it: at the first occurrence of unfamiliar names, Oliver sometimes wrote a phonetic attempt and then corrected it in-line — the name **Coriantumr** (Helaman 1:15), for instance, appears first in a phonetic spelling before being overwritten with the correct, un-English spelling ending in "-mr," which no scribe would produce by ear alone. Thereafter names appear consistently, indicating an initial spelling followed by scribal familiarity.

**No authorial revision.** O shows corrections of scribal slips but essentially none of the second-thought phenomena of composition: no rearranged passages, no deleted false starts of substance, no marginal rewrites. The text runs forward. Skousen estimates from correction patterns and scribal flow that Joseph dictated in increments of **roughly twenty to thirty words** at a time. Whitmer, Harris, and Emma all independently testified that Joseph never asked to have prior text read back before resuming. Emma's 1879 account:

> "I am satisfied that no man could have dictated the writing of the manuscripts unless he was inspired; for, when acting as his scribe, your father would dictate to me hour after hour; and when returning after meals, or after interruptions, he would at once begin where he had left off, without either seeing the manuscript or having any portion of it read to him. This was a usual thing for him to do." ("Last Testimony of Sister Emma," *Saints' Herald* 26, October 1, 1879)

</details>

For a text with hundreds of interlocking names, an internally consistent chronology spanning a millennium, and sustained flashback structures (e.g., the records-within-records of Mosiah–Alma), the absence of retrospection is, as a compositional matter, extraordinary.

**No punctuation.** The dictated manuscript is essentially punctuation-free, running on as speech does. John H. Gilbert, the non-Mormon typesetter, recalled in his 1892 memorandum: "Every chapter, if I remember correctly, was one solid paragraph, without a punctuation mark, from beginning to end," adding that he supplied the punctuation and paragraphing for the 1830 edition himself — testimony given decades later without sympathy for the book's claims, and consistent with the surviving leaves.

## Tight or Loose Control?

The manuscripts have also framed the leading scholarly debate about the translation's nature. Skousen argues for **"tight control"**: that specific words were given to Joseph, evidenced by transmitted spellings and by systematic non-English syntax in the earliest text — most famously Hebrew-like **if–and conditionals** ("if he shall be led away... and it shall be done unto him," original text of 1 Nephi 17:50 and repeatedly in Helaman 12:13–21, 1830 edition), grammatical in Hebrew, ungrammatical in English, and quietly removed in Joseph's 1837 edit. Others, notably Brant Gardner (*The Gift and Power: Translating the Book of Mormon*, 2011), argue for **"loose" or functional control**, with meaning given and language supplied through Joseph's mental lexicon, citing King James phrasing and nineteenth-century idiom in the text. Skousen and Stanford Carmack have further documented Early Modern English syntactic patterns in the earliest text — a finding whose interpretation remains contested. What no party to the debate disputes is the underlying datum: the physical manuscripts record rapid, forward-moving, orally dictated prose, produced exactly as the witnesses said it was.
