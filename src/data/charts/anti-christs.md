Sought out the prophet Jacob directly to “shake me from the faith” (7:5). | Physically imposing (1:2), preached for money, wore “very costly apparel” (1:6), established his own church. When the aged teacher Gideon withstood him, Nehor drew his sword and killed him (1:9). | Cross-examination and bribery: “Will ye answer me a few questions?” (11:21), then “here are six onties of silver, and all these will I give thee if thou wilt deny the existence of a Supreme Being” (11:22). Laid word-traps on the unity of God and the Son (11:26–38), “the foremost to accuse Amulek and Alma” (10:31). | Argument and accusation: charged the priests with glutting “yourselves with the labors of their hands” and keeping the people “in bondage” (30:27–28) — accusing faithful priests of exactly what Nehor had practiced. His preaching led many “to commit whoredoms” (30:18).
- **On scripture and prophets** | Professed belief: “Believest thou the scriptures? And he said, Yea” (7:10) — while denying their central testimony. Jacob: “Then ye do not understand them; for they truly testify of Christ” (7:11). | Preached “that which he termed to be the word of God” (1:3) — kept scriptural language while emptying it of judgment and repentance. | Used doctrine as a snare — his questions about God, the Son, and salvation in sin (11:26–38) were designed to catch, not to learn. After conviction the same questions turn sincere: he “began to inquire of them diligently… concerning the kingdom of God” (12:8). | Rejected them outright as “foolish traditions,” the effect of “a frenzied mind” and “derangement” (30:14, 16).

The only affliction he suffered was self-inflicted: a burning fever “caused by the great tribulations of his mind” (15:3). | Demanded a sign twice (30:43, 45, 48). Alma: “Thou hast had signs enough… all things denote there is a God” (30:44). Korihor was struck dumb (30:49–50).

At Sidom, Alma asked, “Believest thou in the power of Christ unto salvation?” and he was healed according to his faith (15:6–11). | Confessed only after being struck dumb: “I always knew that there was a God. But behold, the devil hath deceived me; for he appeared unto me in the form of an angel” (30:52–53).
- **The end** | Died after his public confession: “when he had said these words he could say no more, and he gave up the ghost” (7:20). | Executed on the hill Manti under the law of Mosiah: “there he suffered an ignominious death” (1:15). | Baptized: “Alma baptized Zeezrom unto the Lord; and he began from that time forth to preach unto the people” (15:12). Years later he is a missionary companion of Alma and Amulek to the Zoramites (31:6). | The curse was not lifted (30:55); he begged house to house and was “run upon and trodden down” among the Zoramites, “even until he was dead” (30:56–59).
- **Aftermath** | “Peace and the love of God was restored again among the people; and they searched the scriptures” (7:23). | His death “did not put an end to the spreading of priestcraft” (1:16); the “order of Nehor” persists through the Amlicites, Amulonites, and the persecutors at Ammonihah. | A life of ministry — the record’s standing proof that the pattern of the others can be broken by repentance. A Nephite city later bears his name (56:14). | “They were all convinced of the wickedness of Korihor; therefore they were all converted again unto the Lord” (30:58). Mormon appends the moral: “the devil will not support his children at the last day” (30:60).

## Patterns across the four

Read in sequence, Sherem, Nehor, and Korihor form an escalation of unbelief. Sherem is a religious conservative who professes belief in the scriptures and the law of Moses but denies the Christ they point to. Nehor keeps God and salvation but removes judgment, repentance, and sacrifice — and monetizes what remains. Korihor discards the entire structure: no Christ, no God that can be known, no atonement, no life after death. Each concedes less than the one before, and each requires a different kind of answer — Jacob answers Sherem from scripture and revelation, Alma answers Nehor with the civil law, and Alma answers Korihor with an argument about evidence, testimony, and the burden of proof.

Several threads bind the accounts together. Sherem and Korihor both demand a sign and receive one they did not want, and in both cases the narrative treats the demand itself as the tell (“what am I that I should tempt God,” Jacob 7:14; “will ye tempt your God?” Alma 30:44). All are ultimately said to have known better: Sherem confesses he “lied unto God,” Nehor acknowledges his teaching was “contrary to the word of God,” Korihor writes “I always knew that there was a God,” and of Zeezrom Amulek says at the outset, “thou knowest that there is a God, but thou lovest that lucre more than him.” The narrators consistently locate the origin of each movement not in honest doubt but in deception and appetite: flattery, riches and honor, and teachings “pleasing unto the carnal mind.”

Zeezrom is the deliberate exception that proves the sequence is about choice rather than fate. He begins further down the same road than Sherem ever went — a paid professional in a Nehorite city, bribing servants of God to deny him — yet he is the only one whose trembling turns to inquiry instead of defiance. Where Sherem and Korihor confess under compulsion and die, Zeezrom confesses freely, pleads for the men he accused, bears the cost of it, and is healed “according to his faith which is in Christ” (Alma 15:10). The same conviction of guilt that destroys the others becomes in him the beginning of conversion — and the record quietly completes the arc by listing him, sixteen chapters later, among the missionaries to the very Zoramites who trampled Korihor (Alma 31:6).

There is also a pointed irony across the middle of the sequence. Korihor’s central accusation — that the priests enrich themselves on the labor of the people and keep them in bondage — is a precise description of the priestcraft Nehor actually introduced and the legal culture of Ammonihah in which Zeezrom worked, but it is aimed at priests of whom the record says the opposite (Alma labored with his own hands, Alma 30:32–34). The Book of Mormon thus presents these figures not only as opponents of the church but as students of one another’s methods — and in Zeezrom, as candidates for its redemption.
