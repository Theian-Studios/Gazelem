# The Translator: Joseph Smith's Preparation and Limitations
* image: the-translator.jpg

## An Unlearned Man

Any assessment of the Book of Mormon's origins must begin with a candid measurement of its dictating author's capacities in 1829. On this point the sources — friendly, hostile, and neutral — are in unusual agreement: Joseph Smith Jr., twenty-three years old, possessed neither the education nor the literary experience that authorship of such a text would ordinarily presuppose.

Joseph's own earliest autobiographical account, his 1832 history (the only history containing his own handwriting), states the matter plainly. Because his family's poverty "required the exertions of all that were able to render any assistance for the support of the Family," he wrote, "we were deprived of the bennifit of an education suffice it to say I was mearly instructtid in reading and writing and the ground rules of Arithmatic which constituted my whole literary acquirements" (Joseph Smith, History, circa Summer 1832, Joseph Smith Papers; original spelling retained). The orthography of the document itself corroborates the claim. Historians estimate Joseph's total formal schooling at roughly three years, scattered across Vermont, New Hampshire, and New York district schools.

His mother, Lucy Mack Smith, remembered him as "a remarkably quiet, well-disposed child," and — in a line that has become central to the historiography — noted that Joseph was "less inclined to the perusal of books than any of the rest of our children, but far more given to meditation and deep study" (Lucy Mack Smith, *Biographical Sketches of Joseph Smith the Prophet*, 1853). She likewise recorded that during the Moroni instruction period the family listened to Joseph describe ancient American inhabitants "with as much ease, seemingly, as if he had spent his whole life with them" — an early indication, in her telling, that his knowledge outran his reading.

## Emma Smith's Testimony

The most probative witness to Joseph's literary limitations is Emma Hale Smith, his first scribe and closest observer. In February 1879, months before her death, Emma gave a formal interview to her son Joseph Smith III, published as "Last Testimony of Sister Emma" (*Saints' Herald* 26, October 1, 1879). Her assessment was categorical:

> "Joseph Smith could neither write nor dictate a coherent and well-worded letter; let alone dictating a book like the Book of Mormon. And, though I was an active participant in the scenes that transpired, and was present during the translation of the plates, and had cognizance of things as they transpired, it is marvelous to me, 'a marvel and a wonder,' as much so as to any one else... My belief is that the Book of Mormon is of divine authenticity — I have not the slightest doubt of it. I am satisfied that no man could have dictated the writing of the manuscripts unless he was inspired; for, when acting as his scribe, your father would dictate to me hour after hour; and when returning after meals, or after interruptions, he would at once begin where he had left off, without either seeing the manuscript or having any portion of it read to him. This was a usual thing for him to do. It would have been improbable that a learned man could do this; and, for one so ignorant and unlearned as he was, it was simply impossible." ("Last Testimony of Sister Emma," *Saints' Herald* 26, October 1, 1879)

</details>

Her conclusion left no room for a naturalistic middle position: "for one so ignorant and unlearned as he was, it was simply impossible."

Emma's testimony carries particular evidentiary weight for three reasons. First, proximity: she served as scribe before Oliver Cowdery's arrival and lived in the small Harmony home where most of the extant text was dictated. Second, timing: the interview was given a half-century after the events, when Emma — estranged from the Utah church, remarried to a non-Mormon, with no institutional incentive — had every social latitude to disclaim the work. Third, corroboration: her account of the mechanics of dictation aligns independently with Whitmer, Harris, and Knight accounts recorded decades apart.

## Hostile Corroboration

Skeptical contemporaries, notably those gathered in E. D. Howe's *Mormonism Unvailed* (1834), sought to discredit Joseph's character but inadvertently confirmed his lack of learning. Isaac Hale, Joseph's disapproving father-in-law, and the Palmyra affidavits describe a young laborer and treasure-seeker — never a reader, writer, or student. This created the explanatory dilemma that has driven naturalistic theories ever since: if Joseph could not have written the book, someone else must have (hence the Spalding-Rigdon theory, now abandoned by nearly all scholars after the discovery of the Spalding manuscript in 1884 and modern textual analysis), or he must have possessed concealed genius (the position of Fawn Brodie in 1945 and, in modified form, later naturalistic biographers). Each theory concedes the central datum: the man observed in 1829 does not account for the text produced in 1829.
